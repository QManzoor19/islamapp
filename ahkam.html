The Legal Verses of the Quran

Every Quranic verse with a legal ruling, in order, with the majority Sunni interpretation and notes where the four schools differ. Roughly 500 verses total — this is the ongoing catalog.

Caveats — read first
  • This is a learning resource, not a fatwa. For binding rulings, consult a qualified scholar.
  • "Majority view" means the position held by all four Sunni schools, or three of them, where they agree. Shia (Jaʿfarī) positions are noted only where they differ significantly.
  • The Quran's legal verses are the foundation of Islamic law, not all of it. Most fiqh comes from interpreting these verses through the lens of Sunnah and scholarly reasoning.
  • This page is being built incrementally. Surah Al-Baqara is the pilot.

Quran 2:43
Prayer Zakat Congregation
وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَارْكَعُوا مَعَ الرَّاكِعِينَ
"Establish prayer, give the zakat, and bow with those who bow."
Plain meaning
Three foundational duties in a single phrase: prayer (individual worship), zakat (social duty), and "bowing with those who bow" — congregational prayer. The verse compresses the outward structure of Islam into one sentence.
Majority view
All four schools cite this as a foundational source for the obligation of prayer, zakat, and the recommendation of congregational prayer. The pairing "establish prayer + give zakat" appears together over 30 times in the Quran — a deliberate emphasis on the inseparable link between worship of God and social responsibility.
Related verses
Q 2:110, Q 9:103 (zakat purifies wealth). Q 9:60 (the eight categories of zakat recipients). Q 24:56 (establish prayer, give zakat, obey the Messenger).
Quran 2:106
Abrogation Meta-principle
مَا نَنسَخْ مِنْ آيَةٍ أَوْ نُنسِهَا نَأْتِ بِخَيْرٍ مِّنْهَا أَوْ مِثْلِهَا ۗ أَلَمْ تَعْلَمْ أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ
"Whatever verse We abrogate or cause to be forgotten, We bring one better or similar. Do you not know that Allah is over all things competent?"
Plain meaning
The Quran acknowledges naskh — abrogation — that some earlier rulings were superseded by later ones. Where this happened, Allah provided an equal or better ruling. Abrogation is not Allah "changing His mind"; it reflects the Quran's gradual revelation over 23 years and the developmental nature of legal reform.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools accept abrogation as a real phenomenon in Quranic legislation. Most-cited examples: (1) the qibla changed from Jerusalem to the Kaʿba (Q 2:144), (2) the gradual prohibition of alcohol culminating in Q 5:90, (3) the original 1-year provision for widows reduced to 4 months 10 days (Q 2:240 → Q 2:234), (4) the conditional permission of wills to heirs replaced by the fixed inheritance shares (Q 2:180 → Q 4:11).
Where the schools differ
Number of abrogated versesClassical scholars cataloged various numbers — as low as ~5 (al-Suyūṭī's minimal count), up to ~50 (older classical works). Modern scholars tend toward smaller numbers, often arguing that what was once classified as abrogation is actually contextual specification.
Naskh of Quran by SunnahḤanafī, Mālikī: possible. Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: only the Quran abrogates the Quran.
Related verses
Q 16:101 (when We replace one sign with another). Q 2:144 (the qibla abrogation in action). Q 13:39 (Allah erases and confirms what He wills).
Quran 2:114
Mosques Worship rights
وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّن مَّنَعَ مَسَاجِدَ اللَّهِ أَن يُذْكَرَ فِيهَا اسْمُهُ وَسَعَىٰ فِي خَرَابِهَا
"Who is more unjust than one who prevents Allah's mosques from His name being mentioned in them, and seeks their destruction?"
Plain meaning
Preventing people from worship in mosques, or destroying mosques, is named as among the greatest injustices. The original context was Quraysh preventing Muslims from praying at the Kaʿba. The verse's principle extends broadly: religious freedom of worship is a sacred right; physical destruction of sacred spaces is condemned.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the principle. Modern application: governments restricting religious practice, vandalism of mosques, demolition of sacred sites. The verse is also cited in classical fiqh of war: religious sites are not to be targeted in legitimate warfare.
Related verses
Q 2:217 (preventing access to the Sacred Mosque). Q 9:18 (those who build and maintain mosques). Q 22:40 (Allah's preservation of churches, synagogues, and mosques alike).
Quran 2:142–144
Qibla Abrogation
قَدْ نَرَىٰ تَقَلُّبَ وَجْهِكَ فِي السَّمَاءِ ۖ فَلَنُوَلِّيَنَّكَ قِبْلَةً تَرْضَاهَا ۚ فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ ۚ وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ
"We have seen your face turning toward the heaven. So We will surely turn you toward a qibla you will be pleased with. Turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque. Wherever you are, turn your faces toward it."
Plain meaning
For about 16–17 months in Medina, the Prophet ﷺ prayed toward Jerusalem. This verse, revealed during the Dhuhr prayer, changed the qibla to the Kaʿba in Mecca — Abraham's original sanctuary. The companions immediately turned mid-prayer. The verse acknowledges the Prophet's longing (his face turning toward heaven, anticipating the change).
Majority view
Universal: the qibla is the Kaʿba. The Jerusalem-orientation was the first instance of explicit naskh in the Quran. The verse continues by noting the Jews and Christians of Medina were upset by the change — but the Quran replies: every group has a direction; outdo each other in good works (2:148).
Where the schools differ
Precision of directionḤanafī: facing the general direction of the Kaʿba is sufficient. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: more precision required where ascertainable. Practical impact: minor for those at distance; modern apps make it moot.
If unsure of directionAll schools: do your best with available signs (stars, sun, asking locals). If you pray in good faith and later learn you were wrong, the prayer counts.
Related verses
Q 2:115 (everywhere is Allah's face). Q 2:148 (every group has a direction). Q 2:177 (righteousness is not about facing east or west).
Quran 2:158
Hajj Saʿy
إِنَّ الصَّفَا وَالْمَرْوَةَ مِن شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ ۖ فَمَنْ حَجَّ الْبَيْتَ أَوِ اعْتَمَرَ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِ أَن يَطَّوَّفَ بِهِمَا
"Indeed, Safa and Marwa are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs Hajj or Umrah — there is no blame on him for moving between them."
Plain meaning
Safa and Marwa are two small hills near the Kaʿba in Mecca. Walking between them seven times (saʿy) commemorates Hagar's frantic search for water for the infant Ishmael. The "no blame" wording addresses companions' hesitation — some thought walking between them was a pagan holdover. The verse confirms it is part of Islamic Hajj and Umrah.
Majority view
All four schools agree saʿy is part of both Hajj and Umrah. The disagreement is on its status — pillar vs. obligatory.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīSaʿy is wājib (obligatory). Missing it can be made up with a sacrificial animal.
Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, ḤanbalīSaʿy is a rukn (pillar) — Hajj is invalid without it. No substitution possible.
Related verses
Q 22:33 (sacred symbols and benefits). Q 2:196 (Hajj completion). Q 5:97 (the Sacred House as a standing institution for people).
Quran 2:168
Food Halal & ṭayyib
يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ كُلُوا مِمَّا فِي الْأَرْضِ حَلَالًا طَيِّبًا وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا خُطُوَاتِ الشَّيْطَانِ ۚ إِنَّهُ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ مُّبِينٌ
"O people, eat from what is on earth that is lawful and good. Do not follow Satan's footsteps — he is to you a clear enemy."
Plain meaning
Two requirements for food: it must be both halal (legally permitted) and tayyib (wholesome, beneficial). Permitted but harmful — junk food, excessive consumption, ethically dubious sourcing — fails the second test. The verse adds: don't follow Satan's path even in eating. Diet is a moral matter.
Majority view
All four schools require both halal and tayyib. Halal is the legal threshold (the meat is properly slaughtered, no pork, no intoxicants, etc.). Tayyib is the ethical refinement: wholesome, not harmful, not associated with cruelty or extravagance. Many classical scholars argued that the second condition (tayyib) creates a higher Islamic standard than minimum legal halal.
Where the schools differ
Scope of "ṭayyib"Some classical: anything not specifically forbidden that doesn't physically harm. Some modern: extends to ethical sourcing, ecological concerns, treatment of animals during life (not just slaughter).
Practical applicationAll agree: pork is haram regardless of "ṭayyib." But legally-halal-but-unhealthy choices (factory-farmed meat, processed food in excess) increasingly debated as falling short of full Quranic standard.
Related verses
Q 2:172 (eat from the good things provided). Q 5:88 (eat lawful good things). Q 7:31–32 (no extravagance in eating; don't forbid what Allah has permitted).
Quran 2:173
Food & drink Necessity
إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلْمَيْتَةَ وَٱلدَّمَ وَلَحْمَ ٱلْخِنزِيرِ وَمَآ أُهِلَّ بِهِۦ لِغَيْرِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ فَمَنِ ٱضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَآ إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ
"He has only forbidden you carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that over which any other than Allah has been invoked. But whoever is forced by necessity — neither desiring nor transgressing — there is no sin upon him. Indeed, Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."
Plain meaning
Four categories are forbidden: (1) carrion — animals that died without proper Islamic slaughter, (2) flowing blood, (3) pork in any form, (4) anything slaughtered in the name of any deity other than Allah. The verse closes with a necessity exemption.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools agree on these four prohibitions. The "necessity" exemption (ḍarūra) is applied narrowly: it permits only what is needed to preserve life, not to enjoy. The principle is encoded in the maxim "necessity makes the forbidden lawful" — but limited by "necessity is measured by its scope."
Where the schools differ
All four schoolsagree on the core prohibitions; differences are at the edges.
Aquatic lifeMālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī permit nearly all sea creatures. Ḥanafī restricts to fish proper.
Fish bloodConsidered exempt from the blood prohibition by all schools (it's blood that hasn't "flowed").
Related verses
Q 5:3 expands the list (strangled, beaten, fallen, gored, partially eaten by wild beasts — unless slaughtered before death). Q 6:145 restates these four. Q 16:115 again.
Quran 2:177
Righteousness Charity Covenants
لَّيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا ۖ وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ ۗ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ
"Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward east or west — but truly righteous is one who believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Scripture, and the Prophets; who gives wealth, despite love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask, and to free slaves; who establishes prayer and gives zakat; who fulfills their covenants when made; and who is patient in poverty, hardship, and during battle. Those are the truthful, and those are the God-conscious."
Plain meaning
A comprehensive definition of true righteousness — not external direction or ritual gesture but inward conviction expressed through specific actions. The verse names six elements: (1) right belief in five articles of faith, (2) generous giving to six categories of recipients, (3) establishing prayer, (4) paying zakat, (5) keeping covenants, (6) patience through hardship and war.
Majority view
One of the densest single verses in the Quran for legal content. The six recipient categories named here (relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask, freeing slaves) are broader than zakat's official eight (Q 9:60) — these describe voluntary giving (ṣadaqa) above the mandatory floor. "Freeing slaves" produced extensive classical jurisprudence on manumission. "Keeping covenants" binds both personal promises and international treaties. All four Sunni schools read this verse consistently — it is one of the strongest Quranic statements that piety is character, not ritual posture.
Where the schools differ
All four schoolsagree on the core: the listed elements are either obligatory (prayer, zakat, covenants) or strongly recommended (extra charity, manumission). No major textual disagreement.
Sāʾil (the one who asks)Ḥanafī: includes anyone who requests aid, even without obvious need. The other three schools: applies to those whose request itself signals need — not arbitrary panhandling.
Charity beyond zakatAll four schools: voluntary giving (ṣadaqa) is recommended, not obligatory. But this verse implies zakat alone is the floor of piety, not the full measure — a frequent classical citation for the moral obligation to give more.
Related verses
Q 2:189 echoes the opening — "righteousness is not entering homes from the back" — both verses correct the idea that piety is about external direction. Q 9:60 lists zakat's eight categories. Q 76:8 echoes "feeding the needy, the orphan, and the captive." This single verse weaves together three key Quranic terms: birr (righteousness), īmān (faith), and taqwā (God-consciousness).
Quran 2:178–179
Criminal — Murder Qiṣāṣ
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْقِصَاصُ فِي الْقَتْلَى ۖ الْحُرُّ بِالْحُرِّ وَالْعَبْدُ بِالْعَبْدِ وَالْأُنْثَىٰ بِالْأُنْثَىٰ ۚ فَمَنْ عُفِيَ لَهُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ شَيْءٌ فَاتِّبَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَأَدَاءٌ إِلَيْهِ بِإِحْسَانٍ ۗ … وَلَكُمْ فِي الْقِصَاصِ حَيَاةٌ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ
"O you who believe, retribution is prescribed for you in the case of murder… But if anyone is pardoned by his brother [the victim's family], let there be a fair demand and a payment with kindness. … In retribution there is life for you, O people of understanding."
Plain meaning
For deliberate murder, retribution (qiṣāṣ) is the default — but the victim's family may forgive in exchange for blood money (diya), or forgive outright. The closing verse explains the wisdom: the threat of being held to account saves lives.
Majority view
Three options after deliberate, unlawful killing: (1) execution by the state, only at the family's request; (2) acceptance of diya; (3) full pardon. The choice belongs to the victim's family, not the state. Forgiveness is consistently encouraged.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīA Muslim can be executed for killing a non-Muslim under Muslim rule (citing the principle of equal protection).
Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, ḤanbalīA Muslim is not executed for killing a non-Muslim, but pays diya and is punished otherwise.
All fouragree that diya for a man is twice the diya for a woman in classical rulings; some modern fatwas have revised this.
Notes
The phrase "free for free, slave for slave, female for female" was descriptive of pre-Islamic vendetta logic, which would escalate by demanding higher-status victims. The verse equalizes by capping retribution at one-for-one.
Quran 2:180
Wills Inheritance
كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ إِن تَرَكَ خَيْرًا الْوَصِيَّةُ لِلْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۖ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُتَّقِينَ
"Prescribed for you, when death approaches one of you who is leaving wealth, is the making of a will for parents and near relatives with kindness — a duty upon the God-conscious."
Plain meaning
When a Muslim is dying and leaves behind wealth, making a will (waṣiyya) for parents and close relatives is prescribed. The wording — "kutiba ʿalaykum" (prescribed for you) — is the Quran's standard language of legal obligation.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools hold this verse was partially abrogated by the fixed inheritance shares of Q 4:11–12, 4:176. After those verses were revealed, the will cannot favor someone already entitled to inheritance (parents, spouse, children) — they receive fixed shares automatically. The will retains scope only for up to one-third of the estate, directed at non-heirs (charities, friends, distant relatives, debts). The famous hadith of Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ established the third-limit: \"One-third — and one-third is plenty.\" (Bukhari, Muslim).
Where the schools differ
All four schoolsagree the inheritance-eligible heirs are excluded from receiving via the will. Disagreement is only at the edges.
Ibn Ḥazm (Ẓāhirī, minority)holds that part of the verse is still operative — that bequests to relatives not already inheriting (e.g., a grandchild whose parent is alive) are still required.
All schoolsagree making a will remains highly recommended even after abrogation. The Prophet ﷺ said: \"It is not permitted for a Muslim with something to bequeath to spend two nights without his will written.\" (Bukhari)
Related verses
Q 4:11–12 and 4:176 (the inheritance verses that triggered the partial abrogation). Q 2:240 (provision for widows — also abrogated by inheritance). Q 5:106–108 (procedural details on witnesses to a deathbed will).
Quran 2:183
Worship — Fasting
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الصِّيَامُ كَمَا كُتِبَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ
"O you who believe, fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you — that you may attain God-consciousness."
Plain meaning
Fasting is made obligatory upon the believers. The verse roots the practice in earlier traditions ("as it was prescribed for those before you") and states its purpose: taqwā — protective awareness of God.
Majority view
The fast referenced here is Ramadan, specified in the next verses (2:184–185). All four schools agree fasting Ramadan is obligatory on every adult, sane, healthy Muslim. The fast runs from dawn (fajr) to sunset (maghrib): no food, drink, smoking, or marital relations during daylight.
Where the schools differ
ExemptionsAll four schools agree on exemptions for travel, illness, menstruation/postpartum bleeding, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and severe old age. Differences are in the make-up rules (qaḍāʾ vs. fidya).
Pregnant/nursing womenḤanafī/Shāfiʿī: make up later. Mālikī/Ḥanbalī (in some cases): feed the poor instead. Modern fatwas vary.
Related verses
Q 2:184 (concessions for the sick/traveler), 2:185 (Ramadan named, the Quran revealed in it), 2:187 (eating until the white thread is distinguishable from the black at dawn; the night allowance with spouses).
Quran 2:184–185
Fasting Exemptions Ramadan
أَيَّامًا مَّعْدُودَاتٍ ۚ فَمَن كَانَ مِنكُم مَّرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِّنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ ۚ وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ ۝ شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ … يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ
"[Fasting is for] a limited number of days. Whoever among you is ill or on a journey — let him fast the same number of other days. For those who can barely fast: feeding a poor person [as expiation]… The month of Ramadan is that in which the Quran was sent down… Allah intends ease for you, not hardship."
Plain meaning
Two verses on fasting that together establish: the exemptions (sick + traveler must make up days later; those who cannot fast at all pay fidya — feeding a poor person per missed day), the month (Ramadan, the month of revelation), and the underlying principle (\"Allah intends ease for you, not hardship\").
Majority view
All four Sunni schools agree on the exemption structure. The temporarily ill and the traveler must make up missed days (qaḍāʾ). The chronically unable substitute fidya (a meal per day). The phrase "yuṭīqūnahu" (literally "those who barely can") is the basis of the fidya exemption — read by classical jurists as those for whom fasting would cause severe hardship: chronic illness, advanced age, certain medical conditions.
Where the schools differ
Pregnant / breastfeeding womenḤanafī, Shāfiʿī: make up the missed days (qaḍāʾ) only. Mālikī, Ḥanbalī (in some cases): if the concern is for the child, also pay fidya. Modern fatwas vary widely.
Traveler's choiceAll four schools agree: fasting while traveling is permitted (and the traveler is rewarded), but breaking and making up later is also permitted. Ḥanafī: easier to fast is preferable. Ẓāhirī (minority): traveler must break.
Fidya amount~half a sāʿ of staple food (rice, wheat, dates) per missed day, or its monetary equivalent. Roughly $5–10 USD modern per day across schools.
Related verses
Q 2:183 (fasting prescribed) — immediately before this. Q 2:187 (fasting hours and intimacy at night) — immediately after. Q 22:78 echoes the "no hardship in religion" principle.
Quran 2:187
Fasting hours Iʿtikāf
أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ لَيْلَةَ الصِّيَامِ الرَّفَثُ إِلَىٰ نِسَائِكُمْ ۚ هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَّكُمْ وَأَنتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَّهُنَّ … وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ ۖ ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ ۚ وَلَا تُبَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَأَنتُمْ عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ
"Permitted to you on the nights of the fast is approaching your wives. They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them… Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct from the black thread; then complete the fast until night. Do not approach them while you are in seclusion (iʿtikāf) in the mosques."
Plain meaning
Three rulings in one verse: (1) marital intimacy is permitted during the nights of Ramadan — abrogating an earlier stricter rule, (2) the fast runs from true dawn (the first horizontal light) to sunset, (3) during iʿtikāf — spiritual retreat in the mosque, typically the last 10 days of Ramadan — marital intimacy is forbidden even at night.
Majority view
The "white thread from black thread" phrase is famously poetic — and was famously misunderstood: hadith records that some companions tried to use literal threads at first. The Prophet ﷺ clarified it means the lightening of the dawn sky. All four schools agree dawn (fajr) begins when the first horizontal light is visible — the start of the fast. Sunset (maghrib) ends it. Iʿtikāf is treated by all schools as a recommended (highly virtuous) practice rather than obligatory.
Where the schools differ
Iʿtikāf durationḤanafī: any length, even part of a day. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: at least one full day, ideally the last 10 days of Ramadan. The Prophet ﷺ did the last 10 days every Ramadan, and 20 days in the year he passed.
Breaking iʿtikāfAll schools agree intimacy breaks it. Schools differ on whether leaving the mosque for valid reasons (illness, urgent need) invalidates it. Ḥanbalī is most permissive of brief exits; Mālikī most strict.
Fajr calculationAll agree on the principle. Modern prayer-time apps use different angles below horizon — typically 15° (Mecca), 18° (most), 19.5° (some) — producing minor practical variation.
Related verses
Q 2:183–185 (fasting prescribed and exemptions). Q 19:11 (iʿtikāf mentioned in Mary's story). The "they are clothing for you" metaphor is one of the Quran's most-cited descriptions of marriage — read by classical scholars as mutual protection, mutual beautification, mutual intimacy.
Quran 2:188
Bribery Property
وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ وَتُدْلُوا بِهَا إِلَى الْحُكَّامِ لِتَأْكُلُوا فَرِيقًا مِّنْ أَمْوَالِ النَّاسِ بِالْإِثْمِ وَأَنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ
"Do not consume one another's wealth unjustly, nor offer it as bribes to judges to consume a portion of others' wealth sinfully — while you know.
Plain meaning
Two prohibitions linked: (1) general unjust consumption of others' wealth, and (2) specifically bribing those in authority to extract what isn't yours. Both the giver and the receiver of bribes are condemned. The closing phrase — "while you know" — frames bribery as conscious wrongdoing, not accidental.
Majority view
Universally agreed across all four schools: bribery is forbidden categorically. The Prophet ﷺ reinforced this with the famous hadith: \"The Messenger of Allah cursed the giver of bribes and the receiver\" (Abu Dawud 3580, Tirmidhi 1336). Many classical and modern jurists extend this to the intermediary who facilitates a bribe — also cursed in some narrations.
Where the schools differ
Necessity defenseSome modern scholars (incl. al-Qaraḍāwī): a person who bribes to access a legitimate right being unjustly withheld is excused — but the recipient is still sinning. Most classical jurists reject even this defense.
Gifts to judges/officialsAll four schools forbid a judge from accepting any gift from a person with a case before him. Schools have detailed criteria to distinguish bribes from legitimate gifts: timing, context, expectation of return.
Scope of "ḥukkām"Schools agree this includes judges, governors, and anyone in decision-making authority. Modern extension: government officials, hiring managers, customs officers — anyone whose decision affects rights.
Related verses
Q 4:29 (consume wealth only by mutual consent). Q 4:58 (return trusts to those due them, and judge with justice). Q 4:135 (justice even against self).
Quran 2:190–191
Defensive war Just war
وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ ۝ … وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ
"Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you — but do not transgress. Allah does not love transgressors… Persecution (fitna) is worse than killing."
Plain meaning
The foundational Quranic statement of just war: (1) fighting is permitted only against those who fight you, (2) "do not transgress" — don't escalate beyond what's necessary or strike non-combatants, (3) persecution (fitna) — preventing people from practicing their faith — is gravely wrong, even justifying organized resistance.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools derive the rules of jus in bello (conduct in war) from this verse. The Prophet ﷺ and his successor Abū Bakr explicitly forbade: killing non-combatants (women, children, elderly, religious figures, farmers), destroying property unnecessarily, harming crops, fruit trees, or livestock beyond need. The classical principle: war must be proportional, discriminating, and defensive in trigger.
Where the schools differ
Offensive war (jihād al-ṭalab)Classical Ḥanafī, Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī jurisprudence permitted offensive jihad under specific conditions (legitimate state authority, declaration to non-Muslims first to accept Islam or pay jizya). Modern scholars across all schools largely reject offensive jihad — only defensive war remains permitted.
"Fitna" scopeḤanafī, Mālikī: religious persecution specifically (preventing worship, forcing apostasy). Shāfiʿī, some moderns: broader political oppression that crushes free practice of faith.
Acceptance of surrenderAll four schools: surrender must be accepted immediately. Even one declaring shahada in the heat of battle is to be spared (a strict Prophetic ruling on this incident).
Related verses
Q 22:39–40 (first permission to fight — the Prophet's first authorization). Q 8:61 (accept peace when offered). Q 60:8 (kindness and justice toward non-combatant non-Muslims). Q 5:32 (one killing is like killing all of humanity).
Quran 2:195
Spending Iḥsān
وَأَنفِقُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا تُلْقُوا بِأَيْدِيكُمْ إِلَى التَّهْلُكَةِ ۛ وَأَحْسِنُوا ۛ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ
"Spend in the way of Allah, and do not throw yourselves into destruction by your own hands. And do good — Allah loves those who do good.
Plain meaning
Three teachings: (1) generous spending in good causes is commanded, (2) "do not throw yourselves into destruction" — failing to spend brings the destruction, not the spending itself, (3) iḥsān (excellence in all action) is what Allah loves.
Majority view
The "destruction" warning has two classical interpretations, both accepted: (1) contextual — failing to fund military defense creates strategic vulnerability (the original wartime setting), (2) spiritual — refusing to give in any cause destroys the soul. Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī, the famous companion, cited the verse on his deathbed to justify continuing to fight in old age rather than stay home. All four schools agree: this verse does not mean "give until destitute" — that reading is constrained by Q 25:67 (moderate spending) and Q 17:29 (don't tie up your hand, but don't spend recklessly either).
Where the schools differ
Scope of "tahluka"All schools agree the destruction warned against is failure to give in good causes — not excess giving. Schools differ in interpretation: Ḥanafī, Mālikī emphasize hoarding as the destruction; Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī also include cowardice in battle.
Iḥsān as obligationAll schools treat iḥsān (excellence) as the third tier of religious life — beyond Islam (outward submission) and Īmān (inward faith). The Hadith of Gabriel defines it: \"to worship Allah as if you see Him; though you do not see Him, He sees you.\" Recommended at the highest level.
Related verses
Q 2:215 (what to spend, and on whom). Q 25:67 (moderate spending — neither extravagant nor stingy). Q 17:26–27 (don't be wasteful, the wasteful are brothers of devils). Q 9:34 (warning against hoarding gold and silver).
Quran 2:189
Calendar Proper conduct
يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْأَهِلَّةِ ۖ قُلْ هِيَ مَوَاقِيتُ لِلنَّاسِ وَالْحَجِّ ۗ وَلَيْسَ الْبِرُّ بِأَن تَأْتُوا الْبُيُوتَ مِن ظُهُورِهَا وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنِ اتَّقَىٰ ۗ وَأْتُوا الْبُيُوتَ مِنْ أَبْوَابِهَا
"They ask you about the new moons. Say: They are timings for people and for Hajj. Righteousness is not entering houses from behind, but righteousness is one who is God-conscious. So enter houses through their doors."
Plain meaning
Two teachings in one verse: (1) the lunar crescent moons mark time — for daily affairs and for Hajj, (2) entering one's own home from the back (a pre-Islamic ritual during the Hajj season) was wrongly thought to be piety. The verse corrects: real piety is taqwa, not external custom. Approach things by their proper means.
Majority view
All four schools agree: the Islamic calendar is lunar, and Hajj timing must follow the moon. The "doors" metaphor became a famous classical legal principle — "approach matters by their proper means" — invoked against shortcut-rituals and devotion empty of substance. The verse sits at the boundary of two domains: law (calendar) and ethics (the substance of piety).
Where the schools differ
Calendar useAll four schools require lunar calendar for religious dates (Hajj, Ramadan, ʿEid). For non-religious matters, all schools accept solar calendars in practice.
Moon sighting vs. calculationClassical position: physical sighting is required. Modern position (Saudi Arabia, Muslim World League): astronomical calculation is acceptable. Schools and bodies differ widely; varies by country.
Related verses
Q 9:36 (twelve months, four sacred). Q 5:97 (Hajj timing as part of religion). Q 36:39 (the moon as a sign).
Quran 2:196
Hajj ʿUmrah Iḥṣār
وَأَتِمُّوا الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ لِلَّهِ ۚ فَإِنْ أُحْصِرْتُمْ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ ۖ وَلَا تَحْلِقُوا رُءُوسَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْهَدْيُ مَحِلَّهُ
"Complete Hajj and Umrah for Allah. If you are prevented from completing them, then offer whatever sacrificial animal is feasible. Do not shave your heads until the offering reaches its place of sacrifice."
Plain meaning
Three rulings: (1) once Hajj or Umrah is begun, it must be completed for Allah's sake — not abandoned mid-ritual, (2) if prevented by illness or hostile circumstances (iḥṣār), a sacrificial animal must be offered to exit ihram, (3) the haircut marking the end of ihram cannot happen until the sacrifice is made.
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse as foundational on Hajj completion. The "prevention" clause was revealed at al-Ḥudaybiyya — when the Prophet ﷺ and 1,400 companions were prevented from entering Mecca for Umrah by Quraysh. They sacrificed their animals there and shaved their heads, demonstrating the ruling.
Where the schools differ
Where to sacrifice if preventedḤanafī, Mālikī: must be at the Sacred Precinct (Ḥaram). Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: may be slaughtered at the place of being prevented (citing Ḥudaybiyya).
Tamattuʿ (combining ʿUmrah and Hajj)All four permit it. Procedures differ on exit timing and required sacrifice. Ḥanafī uniquely requires a stricter mode of combination.
Hair removalAll schools: shaving (men) or trimming (men and women). Shaving is preferred for men; women trim a fingertip-length only.
Related verses
Q 3:97 (Hajj for those with means). Q 22:27–28 (Hajj's purposes). Q 48:27 (the prophecy of completing the prevented ʿUmrah).
Quran 2:197
Hajj conduct Iḥrām
الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَّعْلُومَاتٌ ۚ فَمَن فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ
"Hajj is in well-known months. Whoever undertakes Hajj in them — there shall be no sexual intimacy, no wrongdoing, and no quarreling during Hajj."
Plain meaning
Hajj is performed in specific months — Shawwāl, Dhū al-Qaʿda, and the first 10 days of Dhū al-Ḥijja. While in iḥrām, three things are forbidden: rafath (sexual intimacy, including its preliminaries and lewd speech), fusūq (sin and wrongdoing), and jidāl (quarreling).
Majority view
The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever performs Hajj without rafath or sinning returns as on the day his mother bore him" (Bukhari, Muslim). All four schools agree on the three prohibitions. They distinguish between things that simply invalidate iḥrām (intimacy with one's spouse before the first tahallul) and things that diminish the reward (arguing without sinning).
Where the schools differ
"Jidāl" scopeḤanafī, Ḥanbalī (strict): any argument during Hajj is forbidden. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī: only sinful arguments — defending oneself respectfully is acceptable.
Consequence of intimacyIf intercourse occurs before the first tahallul (partial exit from ihram): Hajj is invalidated. The pilgrim must continue, then perform a substitute Hajj the following year, plus sacrifice a large animal (camel).
Related verses
Q 5:1 (do not violate the sacred symbols of Allah). Q 5:95 (no hunting in iḥrām). Q 2:198 (commerce during Hajj is permitted — corrected a misunderstanding that Hajj forbade it).
Quran 2:198
Hajj Commerce
لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَن تَبْتَغُوا فَضْلًا مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ ۚ فَإِذَا أَفَضْتُم مِّنْ عَرَفَاتٍ فَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ عِندَ الْمَشْعَرِ الْحَرَامِ
"It is no sin upon you to seek bounty from your Lord [during Hajj]. When you depart from Arafat, remember Allah at the sacred monument."
Plain meaning
Two rulings: (1) trade and earning a livelihood during Hajj season is permitted — some pre-Islamic Arabs thought it sinful, the verse corrects, (2) after the standing at Arafat (the peak day of Hajj) ends at sunset on 9 Dhū al-Ḥijja, pilgrims must move to Muzdalifa to spend the night and remember Allah at al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām.
Majority view
All schools agree commerce during Hajj is permitted — the historical Hajj season included a major trade fair. The night at Muzdalifa is a recognized rite; schools differ on whether it is a pillar or obligatory.
Where the schools differ
Muzdalifa statusShāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: pillar — required for valid Hajj. Ḥanafī, Mālikī: obligatory (wājib) — may be substituted with sacrifice if missed.
Minimum stay at MuzdalifaSchools agree at least a portion of the night counts. Latest valid stay: pre-dawn of 10 Dhū al-Ḥijja in most schools.
Related verses
Q 2:197 (Hajj conduct). Q 2:199 (proceed from where the people proceed). Q 22:28 (witness benefits at Hajj).

Majority view
All schools cite this as foundational. Classical interpretations vary: (1) bring every aspect of life — work, family, politics — under Islam, or (2) don't mix Islam with elements of other religions, or (3) abandon partial obedience for total. Related verses
Q 5:3 ("today I have perfected your religion for you"). Q 33:36 (no choice for the believer when Allah and His Messenger have decided). Q 2:85 ("do you believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part?").
Quran 2:215
Charity Family first
يَسْأَلُونَكَ مَاذَا يُنفِقُونَ ۖ قُلْ مَا أَنفَقْتُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍ فَلِلْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۗ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ
"They ask you what to spend. Say: Whatever good you spend should go to parents, close relatives, orphans, the needy, and the traveler. Whatever good you do — Allah is fully aware of it."
Plain meaning
Direct answer to: who should I give to? The order is hierarchical: parents first, then close relatives, then strangers in need (orphans, the poor, stranded travelers). Voluntary charity (ṣadaqa) should start with those closest to you whose situation you can directly see.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the hierarchy. The verse establishes a strong principle: don't ignore family in need while donating to faceless distant causes. Parents in particular have an absolute claim — the Prophet ﷺ said: "Your mother, then your mother, then your mother, then your father." Modern relevance: someone whose elderly parent is struggling financially should not be funding aid abroad while neglecting them.
Where the schools differ
Zakat vs. voluntary givingZakat has its own 8 categories (Q 9:60) — and zakat cannot generally go to immediate family (parents, children) since you are already obliged to support them. This verse is for voluntary giving (ṣadaqa), where the family-first principle dominates.
Distant orphan vs. near relativeSchools agree near relative comes first, all else being equal. But if the orphan's need is severe and the relative's is mild, the more acute need takes priority.
Related verses
Q 2:177 (the "righteousness" verse — same six categories of recipient). Q 17:26 (give relatives their due). Q 9:60 (zakat's eight categories, distinct from this voluntary list).

Defensive military action is sometimes prescribed even when distasteful. The verse acknowledges that the human heart naturally recoils from violence — and then offers a wider principle: feelings about an action don't determine its rightness. Sometimes what you dislike is good for you; sometimes what you love is bad.
Majority view
All schools agree — defensive fighting is obligatory when legitimate conditions are met. The Quran consistently frames war as defensive in trigger (you fight those who fight you, Q 2:190) and limited in conduct (no transgression). The wisdom verse — "perhaps you dislike something while it is good" — has been cited in many other contexts beyond war: tests of patience, painful but beneficial medicine, hard work that yields fruit.

When fighting becomes obligatoryClassical fiqh: when a legitimate authority calls for defensive war and the community can field a sufficient force. Modern: self-defense is always permitted; offensive expansion requires very specific justification that few contemporary scholars accept.

Plain meaning
Fighting during the sacred months (Muḥarram, Rajab, Dhū al-Qaʿda, Dhū al-Ḥijja) is normally forbidden. But it is not the worst possible evil. Majority view
All schools agree the sacred-month prohibition stands as a general rule. Modern Muslim ethicists cite this verse for the principle that no rule is absolute when persecution (fitna) is the alternative.

Related verses
Q 9:36 (twelve months, four sacred). Q 5:97 (Hajj timing). Q 2:194 (sacred-for-sacred reciprocity).
Quran 2:219
Intoxicants Gambling
يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ ۖ قُلْ فِيهِمَا إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِن نَّفْعِهِمَا

Plain meaning
An early-stage ruling: the verse acknowledges that wine and gambling do bring some benefit (social, economic) but the harm outweighs it. This is not yet a full prohibition — it's a discouragement that softened the ground for the later total ban.
Majority view
All four schools agree this verse was part of a gradual prohibition. The final, total ban came in Q 5:90–91 ("avoid it — perhaps you will be successful"). The Companions famously poured wine into the streets of Medina the moment Q 5:90 was revealed.
Where the schools differ
Definition of khamrḤanafī (classical): only fermented grape juice is khamr proper; other intoxicants are forbidden by analogy (qiyās). The other three schools: any intoxicating substance is khamr.
Practical outcomeAll four schools forbid all intoxicants. The disagreement is only over why they are forbidden — directly or by analogy.
GamblingAll four agree this includes lottery, casino games, betting on uncertain outcomes — anything where wealth changes hands by chance. Modern: extends to most insurance with speculative elements (debated for actual insurance).
Notes
A classic example of the Quran's gradual reform — three verses across the Meccan and Medinan periods stepped up the warning until total prohibition was set. Used by jurists to argue that legal reform is best done in stages.
Quran 2:220
Orphans Trusteeship

Plain meaning
Guardians managing an orphan's wealth must do what's best for the orphan, not themselves. Household-mixing (sharing meals, joint expenses) is fine — they're family. But intent matters: Allah knows whether you're caretaking or exploiting. The earlier verses (4:2, 4:10) had made guardians anxious about even mixing households; this verse clarifies the line — mixing for the orphan's benefit is fine, mixing to consume their wealth is condemned.
Majority view
All four schools require strict trusteeship of an orphan's wealth. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The one who looks after an orphan and I will be like these two on the Day of Resurrection" (gesturing with two fingers — Bukhari 6005). The guardian must preserve the wealth, grow it where possible, and hand it over when the orphan reaches maturity.
Where the schools differ
Age of "maturity"Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: 15 years lunar age, or earlier with proven sound judgment. Ḥanafī: earlier in some cases — but always with proven competence.
Guardian's compensationIf poor, guardian may take a reasonable wage from the orphan's wealth for the work of management (Q 4:6). If well-off, should not take anything.
Related verses
Q 4:2 (give orphans their wealth). Q 4:6 (test orphans before giving full possession). Q 4:10 (those who eat orphans' wealth eat fire). Q 6:152 (don't approach orphans' wealth except for good).

Marriage Interfaith
وَلَا تَنكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكَاتِ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنَّ ۚ وَلَأَمَةٌ مُّؤْمِنَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِّن مُّشْرِكَةٍ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ ۗ وَلَا تُنكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنُوا
"Do not marry polytheist women until they believe. A believing slave woman is better than a polytheist, however much she may attract you. And do not marry [your women to] polytheist men until they believe."
Plain meaning
Categorical: Muslims may not marry polytheists (mushrikīn) of either gender. The verse explicitly says faith outweighs worldly attraction. Applies to both directions of intermarriage.
Majority view
All four schools hold this verse remains operative. It is read in conjunction with Q 5:5, which permits Muslim men to marry chaste women from "the People of the Book" (Jews and Christians) — so the prohibition in 2:221 is specifically about polytheists. Muslim women may not marry non-Muslim men of any kind by Sunni consensus, regardless of People-of-the-Book status. The reasoning: a non-Muslim husband would not honor his wife's faith, prayer, scripture, or Prophet — placing her religion under strain.
Where the schools differ
Modern atheists/agnosticsSome classify them as mushrik (since denying God is rejection of tawḥīd, the foundational reality). Others place them in a separate category, generally with stricter caution than People of the Book. No Sunni school permits Muslim women to marry them.
Hindus, Buddhists, othersClassical fiqh generally classified them as polytheists due to multi-deity worship. Some modern scholars distinguish — but the practical fatwa remains: marriage with them is not permitted in either direction.
Children's religionAll four Sunni schools: religion passes through the father. Children of a Muslim father are Muslim by default. Children of a non-Muslim father — even if the mother is Muslim — are not Muslim by lineage (one reason for the asymmetry).
Related verses
Q 5:5 (Muslim men permitted to marry chaste People of the Book). Q 60:10 (after Hudaybiyya, a believing woman who emigrates is not returned to her non-Muslim husband). Q 4:25 (provisions for marriage with believing slaves when free women aren't accessible).
Quran 2:222
Menstruation Intimacy
وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْمَحِيضِ ۖ قُلْ هُوَ أَذًى فَاعْتَزِلُوا النِّسَاءَ فِي الْمَحِيضِ ۖ وَلَا تَقْرَبُوهُنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَطْهُرْنَ ۖ فَإِذَا تَطَهَّرْنَ فَأْتُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ
"They ask you about menstruation. Say: It is harm. So withdraw from women during menstruation, and do not approach them until they are purified. When they have purified themselves, approach them as Allah has ordered."
Plain meaning
Marital intercourse is suspended during a woman's period. She is not "untouchable" in any wider sense — daily life, affection, eating together, sleeping in the same bed — all permitted. Only intercourse specifically is forbidden. The verse describes menstruation as adhā (harm/discomfort), not impurity in a polluting sense.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the suspension of intercourse. The Prophet ﷺ explicitly rejected the older Jewish/Zoroastrian practice of isolating menstruating women — when companions asked, he replied: "Do everything except intercourse" (Muslim 302). A menstruating woman is a full marriage partner in every other way. After the period ends, she must perform ghusl (the full ritual bath) before intercourse can resume.
Where the schools differ
Maximum period lengthḤanafī: 10 days maximum. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: 15 days. Bleeding beyond is treated as istiḥāḍa (irregular bleeding), with prayer and fasting resumed.
Physical contact during periodḤanafī, Mālikī: contact above the waistcloth (or below the knees) is permitted; nothing else. Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: any contact other than intercourse is permitted (citing the Prophet's reclining with ʿĀʾisha during her period).
Worship during menstruationAll schools: no prayer, no fasting, no touching the Quran, no entering the mosque, no tawāf. Missed prayers are not made up; missed fasts are made up later.
Related verses
Q 2:223 (intimacy outside menstruation). Q 4:43 (ghusl required for major impurity). Q 65:4 (waiting period for menopausal women — no menstrual cycles to count).
Quran 2:223
Marital intimacy
نِسَاؤُكُمْ حَرْثٌ لَّكُمْ فَأْتُوا حَرْثَكُمْ أَنَّىٰ شِئْتُمْ ۖ وَقَدِّمُوا لِأَنفُسِكُمْ ۚ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ
"Your wives are a tilth for you, so come to your tilth as you wish. Put forth good for yourselves. Be conscious of Allah."
Plain meaning
After the menstruation suspension (2:222), intimacy resumes. The verse permits variety in the position and timing of marital relations. The agricultural metaphor — ḥarth (tilth, the place of cultivation) — specifies the place where conception happens: i.e., the vaginal, not anal, location.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools, unanimously: anal intercourse is forbidden. The "tilth" metaphor specifically refers to the place from which children come. The verse permits variety in position; the metaphor specifies the place. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Cursed is whoever has anal intercourse with his wife" (Abu Dawud 2162, Tirmidhi 135). Universal agreement across all schools.
Where the schools differ
UniversalNo school permits anal intercourse. The Quran's metaphor + hadith elaboration leaves no scholarly dispute.
"Put forth good"Schools interpret variously: pray two rakʿāt before intimacy (some), recite the dua before intercourse (Bukhari narrates the duʿāʾ — "Bismillāh, Allāhumma jannibnā ash-shayṭān…"), seek good offspring through the union.
Related verses
Q 2:222 (menstruation suspension). Q 30:21 (marriage as tranquility and mercy). Q 4:19 (treat wives with kindness).
Quran 2:224–225
Oaths
وَلَا تَجْعَلُوا اللَّهَ عُرْضَةً لِّأَيْمَانِكُمْ أَن تَبَرُّوا وَتَتَّقُوا وَتُصْلِحُوا بَيْنَ النَّاسِ ۗ … لَّا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِن يُؤَاخِذُكُم بِمَا كَسَبَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ
"Do not make Allah a means to your oaths against doing good, being righteous, or reconciling between people. … Allah will not hold you accountable for thoughtless oaths, but only for what your hearts have deliberately committed."
Plain meaning
Two rulings: (1) don't use oaths as an excuse to avoid doing good — e.g., "I swore I would never speak to him again" then used to refuse reconciliation. Break the oath, pay the expiation, do the right thing. (2) Casual oath-language in everyday speech ("by God") does not bind. Only deliberate, committed oaths do.
Majority view
All four schools agree on both rulings. Breaking an oath that prevents you from a good action is preferred to keeping it — the Prophet ﷺ said: "If you swear an oath and then see something better, do what is better and expiate for your oath" (Bukhari). Casual habit-speech ("by God I'm tired") doesn't trigger expiation.
Where the schools differ
What binds an oathḤanafī: explicit invocation of Allah's name plus intent to bind. Mālikī: any clear oath-formula by Allah's name. Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: similar but with slightly different formulas accepted. All schools: pure habit-speech doesn't bind.
Expiation if broken intentionallyUniversal across schools (per Q 5:89): feed 10 poor people, or clothe them, or free a slave; if unable, fast 3 days.
Related verses
Q 5:89 (oath-breaking expiation in detail). Q 16:91 (don't break confirmed oaths). Q 66:2 (Allah ordained the dissolution of certain oaths).
Quran 2:226–227
Ila Divorce
لِّلَّذِينَ يُؤْلُونَ مِن نِّسَائِهِمْ تَرَبُّصُ أَرْبَعَةِ أَشْهُرٍ ۖ فَإِن فَاءُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ ۝ وَإِنْ عَزَمُوا الطَّلَاقَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ
"For those who swear to abstain from their wives, there is a waiting period of four months. If they reconcile — Allah is Forgiving, Merciful. If they resolve on divorce — Allah is Hearing, Knowing."
Plain meaning
Addresses īlāʾ — a pre-Islamic abuse where a husband swore off his wife sexually but did not divorce her, leaving her trapped in legal limbo for years. The Quran imposes a strict 4-month deadline: reconcile or divorce. No woman left as "neither married nor free."
Majority view
All four schools agree on the 4-month deadline. They differ on what happens after it lapses without reconciliation. The verse abolished a major form of wife-abandonment that existed in pre-Islamic Arabia.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīAfter 4 months without reconciliation, divorce becomes automatic and irrevocable (bāʾin). The wife immediately enters her ʿidda.
Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, ḤanbalīAfter 4 months, the wife may petition a judge. If the husband still refuses to reconcile or divorce, the judge dissolves the marriage. Not automatic.
Related verses
Q 2:228 (idda after divorce). Q 4:35 (marital arbitration before divorce). Q 65:1–2 (procedural details of divorce).
Quran 2:228
ʿIdda Wives' rights
وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ ۚ … وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۚ وَلِلرِّجَالِ عَلَيْهِنَّ دَرَجَةٌ
"Divorced women shall wait three menstrual cycles before remarrying. … Women have rights equal to their obligations, according to what is reasonable. Yet men have a degree above them."
Plain meaning
Two foundational rulings in a single verse: (1) the ʿidda — three menstrual cycles divorced women must wait before remarrying, (2) the wives' rights equity principle — wives have rights mirroring their obligations. The "degree" men have is most commonly read as the burden of financial maintenance (Q 4:34).
Majority view
All schools agree on the three-cycle waiting period. Purpose: confirm there's no pregnancy, allow a reconciliation window during which the husband may revoke a revocable divorce, and ensure clear paternity if the woman remarries quickly. For women who don't menstruate (pre-puberty, post-menopause), Q 65:4 specifies three months instead.
Where the schools differ
"Qurūʾ" interpretationḤanafī, Ḥanbalī: menstrual periods (the bleeding itself). Mālikī, Shāfiʿī: purity periods between menstruations. Practical difference: ~3 vs ~2 months in effect.
The "degree"Classical and modern majority: refers to the husband's financial burden (maintenance, dowry, primary responsibility for the family). Not a statement of intrinsic superiority. Modern feminist scholars debate; many traditional scholars hold the financial reading firmly.
Pregnant divorceeAll four schools agree: ʿidda ends at delivery (Q 65:4), not three cycles.
Related verses
Q 65:1–7 (the divorce surah — procedural details). Q 2:234 (widow's ʿidda — 4 months 10 days, different). Q 4:34 (qawwāmūn — the "degree" elaborated).
Quran 2:229
Divorce Khulʿ
الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ ۖ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ ۗ … فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ
"Divorce may be twice — then either retain with kindness or release with dignity. … If you fear they cannot maintain Allah's limits, there is no blame on either of them in what she gives as ransom [to be released]."
Plain meaning
Two distinct rulings: (1) a revocable divorce (ṭalāq rajʿī) can happen twice — within each ʿidda the husband may revoke. After the third, it becomes irrevocable. (2) Khulʿ — woman-initiated divorce: if she cannot continue, she may return the mahr as compensation and be released.
Majority view
All four schools agree on both rulings. The twice-revocable, third-irrevocable structure was designed to limit impulsive divorces. Khulʿ allows a woman in an irretrievably broken marriage to exit even without the husband's free consent — she returns the mahr (or part of it) to compensate.
Where the schools differ
Khulʿ procedureḤanafī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: husband's agreement is needed (though court pressure may compel). Mālikī: similar but somewhat more flexible.
Modern court-ordered khulʿEgypt (2000), Pakistan, others have laws allowing the court to grant khulʿ without husband's consent if the wife clearly cannot bear the marriage. Strongly contested by traditional scholars but practically established.
Triple divorce in one sittingḤanafī, Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī (classical): three divorces stated together count as three. Many modern scholars and some countries: count as one revocable divorce. Major contemporary fiqh debate.
Related verses
Q 2:230 (rule after the third divorce). Q 4:128 (settlement in marital discord). Q 65:1–2 (procedure of divorce).
Quran 2:230
Third divorce
فَإِن طَلَّقَهَا فَلَا تَحِلُّ لَهُ مِن بَعْدُ حَتَّىٰ تَنكِحَ زَوْجًا غَيْرَهُ ۗ فَإِن طَلَّقَهَا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَن يَتَرَاجَعَا
"If he divorces her [the third time], she is not lawful to him afterward until she marries another husband. Then if [the other] divorces her, there is no blame on them returning to each other…"
Plain meaning
After three irrevocable divorces, the original couple cannot remarry until (1) the woman has genuinely married another man, (2) that marriage has been consummated, (3) it has ended naturally (divorce or death). Designed as the final stop on the cycle of repeated divorce — a heavy consequence that discourages reaching three.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the rule. The new marriage must be genuine. The Prophet ﷺ cursed the muḥallil — a man hired purely to marry, consummate, and divorce so that the original couple could remarry. Such contrived "ḥalāla" marriages are condemned across all schools as a fraud on the divine law.
Where the schools differ
Consummation required?All four schools: yes, actual sexual consummation is required, not just contract. Schools differ slightly on evidence standards.
If the intermediate marriage was secretly contrivedHadith condemns the muḥallil. All four schools hold a marriage with the explicit intent of being temporary (to "ḥalāla" the woman back to her first husband) is invalid. Practiced in some communities anyway; rejected in fiqh.
Related verses
Q 2:229 (the twice-revocable structure). Q 65:1–2 (when divorce takes effect).
Quran 2:231
Divorce No harm
وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ ۚ وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِّتَعْتَدُوا ۚ وَمَن يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ
"When you divorce women and they reach their term, either retain them in kindness or release them in kindness. Do not retain them to harm them, transgressing the limits. Whoever does so has wronged himself."
Plain meaning
At the end of the ʿidda, the husband must either take her back genuinely (with real intention to maintain the marriage) or let her go cleanly. The pre-Islamic abuse — revoke the divorce just to prevent her from remarrying, then divorce again, repeatedly — is explicitly forbidden. Maliciously retaining a wife in marriage limbo is condemned as self-destructive.
Majority view
All four schools: universal prohibition on revocation-for-harm. A revocation made without genuine intent to maintain the marriage is, depending on the school, either invalid or sinful even if technically valid. The verse continues: "do not take Allah's verses as a jest" — treating the divorce-and-revocation cycle as a game is severely warned against.
Where the schools differ
Validity of harmful revocationMālikī, Ḥanbalī: a revocation made purely to harm may be challenged in court — wife can seek dissolution. Ḥanafī, Shāfiʿī: revocation is legally valid even with bad intent, but the man is sinful and accountable to Allah.
Modern court interventionMost contemporary Muslim-majority countries: family courts can dissolve such marriages on the wife's petition when malicious retention is shown.
Related verses
Q 2:229 (twice-revocable structure). Q 2:232 (don't prevent divorced women from remarrying). Q 65:1–2 (procedure of divorce with witnesses).
Quran 2:232
Remarriage Wife's choice
وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَلَا تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ أَن يَنكِحْنَ أَزْوَاجَهُنَّ إِذَا تَرَاضَوْا بَيْنَهُم بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
"When you divorce women and they reach their term, do not prevent them from remarrying their former husbands, if both agree on fair terms."
Plain meaning
After a divorce ends and the ʿidda completes, family members — guardians, brothers, fathers — must not block the woman from remarrying her ex-husband if both parties freely agree. The verse was revealed when a man tried to prevent his sister from remarrying her ex-husband out of pride. The woman's choice prevails over family pressure.
Majority view
All four schools: a guardian (walī) cannot unjustly block a woman's remarriage. The Prophet ﷺ even commanded the man in the incident to allow it. The verse establishes a principle of female autonomy in marriage choice — the family supports, but does not veto.
Where the schools differ
Walī requirementMālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: a walī (typically father, then close male relatives) is required for marriage to be valid — but the walī cannot unjustly block. Ḥanafī (uniquely): adult women may marry without a walī, though it is recommended.
"Unjust" blockingAll schools: blocking for petty reasons (pride, hostility) is forbidden. Blocking for substantive reasons (unsuitable spouse, financial concerns) is permitted within limits.
Related verses
Q 2:230 (after third divorce, can't remarry without an intermediate marriage). Q 4:19 (do not inherit women against their will). Q 24:32 (marry the single among you).
Quran 2:233
Breastfeeding Paternal duty Milk-kinship
وَالْوَالِدَاتُ يُرْضِعْنَ أَوْلَادَهُنَّ حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ ۖ لِمَنْ أَرَادَ أَن يُتِمَّ الرَّضَاعَةَ ۚ وَعَلَى الْمَوْلُودِ لَهُ رِزْقُهُنَّ وَكِسْوَتُهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
"Mothers shall breastfeed their children for two complete years — for those who wish to complete the nursing. The father is responsible for the mother's food and clothing during this time, with kindness."
Plain meaning
Several rulings: (1) two years of breastfeeding is the recommended ideal — not a minimum or strict requirement, (2) the father is financially responsible for the nursing mother — even after divorce, even if she has remarried in some readings, (3) weaning earlier is allowed by mutual consent, (4) a wet-nurse may be hired with mutual consent.
Majority view
All four schools: two years is recommended, not obligatory. The father's financial obligation during nursing is firm. Most importantly, this verse establishes the legal category of milk-kinship (riḍāʿa): a child breastfed by another woman becomes mahram (forbidden in marriage and exempt from hijab) to her, her husband, and her direct relatives, exactly as if a blood relative.
Where the schools differ
Milk-kinship thresholdḤanafī, Mālikī: any amount of breastfeeding establishes the relationship. Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: five separate satisfying nursings within the first 2 years.
Custody (ḥaḍāna) after divorceAll schools give the mother priority for very young children. Hanafi: until age 7 (boys), 9 (girls). Maliki: until puberty (girls). Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: until ~7, then child chooses.
Wet-nursing compensationIf the mother declines to nurse, all schools: father may hire a wet-nurse. If mother nurses post-divorce: father pays her like a wet-nurse.
Related verses
Q 65:6 (post-divorce maintenance for nursing mothers). Q 4:23 (foster mothers — milk-relatives — listed among prohibited marriage partners). Q 31:14 (gestation + weaning explicitly named as "in 30 months").
Quran 2:234
Widow's ʿidda Mourning
وَالَّذِينَ يُتَوَفَّوْنَ مِنكُمْ وَيَذَرُونَ أَزْوَاجًا يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنفُسِهِنَّ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَعَشْرًا ۖ فَإِذَا بَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا فَعَلْنَ فِي أَنفُسِهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
"Those of you who die and leave widows — the widows shall wait four months and ten days. Once they reach the end of their term, there is no blame on you for what they do reasonably for themselves."
Plain meaning
A widow's waiting period is four months and ten days — longer than a divorcee's three menstrual cycles. During this time she does not remarry, observes mourning conduct, and remains in her marital home. After it ends she may freely remarry.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the duration. Purposes: confirm absence of pregnancy, provide a dignified mourning period, and honor the marriage that ended. For a pregnant widow, Q 65:4 overrides — her ʿidda ends at delivery, however long that takes.
Where the schools differ
Staying in marital homeḤanafī, Ḥanbalī: required to remain in the marital home during ʿidda. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī: preferred but flexibility allowed if there is genuine need (work, danger, no support).
Mourning conduct (iḥdād)All four schools: the widow refrains from adornment — no perfume, jewelry, fancy clothing, or new engagement during the ʿidda. Differences are in detail.
Pregnant widowAll four: ʿidda ends at delivery, even if delivery comes before four months (Q 65:4).
Related verses
Q 2:228 (divorcee's three-cycle ʿidda). Q 65:4 (pregnant women — ʿidda ends at delivery). Q 2:235 (hints at proposing to widows during ʿidda — only indirectly).
Quran 2:235
Proposing in ʿidda Widows
وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا عَرَّضْتُم بِهِ مِنْ خِطْبَةِ النِّسَاءِ أَوْ أَكْنَنتُمْ فِي أَنفُسِكُمْ ۚ … وَلَٰكِن لَّا تُوَاعِدُوهُنَّ سِرًّا إِلَّا أَن تَقُولُوا قَوْلًا مَّعْرُوفًا
"There is no blame on you for what you hint at of marriage proposals to women [in their ʿidda] or what you keep within yourselves. … But do not make any secret promise to them — except to speak in honor."
Plain meaning
During a widow's ʿidda (4 months 10 days), a man cannot make an explicit marriage proposal — but he may hint at his interest. Direct private arrangements are forbidden; only honorable, indirect expressions are permitted. The wait protects the recently-bereaved from being immediately re-courted.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the distinction: hinting (taʿrīḍ) is permitted, explicit proposal (taṣrīḥ) is forbidden during ʿidda. The principle: respect the mourning period, but don't deny her the awareness that future possibilities exist.
Where the schools differ
Hinting vs explicitḤanafī (strictest): even saying "I am interested in marrying you when your ʿidda ends" crosses the line. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: such direct expressions of future intent are permitted; only present-tense explicit proposals are forbidden.
Permitted hints"You are a good woman" or "I hope to be married soon" — these are universally accepted as permissible.
Related verses
Q 2:228 (divorcee's ʿidda). Q 2:234 (widow's ʿidda — 4 months 10 days). Q 65:4 (pregnant women's ʿidda).
Quran 2:236–237
Pre-consummation divorce Mahr
لَّا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِن طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ مَا لَمْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ أَوْ تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً ۚ وَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ … وَإِن طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِن قَبْلِ أَن تَمَسُّوهُنَّ وَقَدْ فَرَضْتُمْ لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً فَنِصْفُ مَا فَرَضْتُمْ
"No blame on you if you divorce women before having consummated [the marriage] or stipulating a mahr — but give them a fair provision. … If you divorce them before consummation, having stipulated a mahr, then half of what was stipulated."
Plain meaning
Two scenarios for pre-consummation divorce: (1) no mahr stipulated → give mutʿa (a fair provision proportional to the husband's means), (2) mahr stipulated but unconsumated → pay half the stipulated mahr. The woman is compensated either way. The verse continues that either spouse may forgive the other their portion — generosity is preferred.
Majority view
All four schools agree on both rulings. The half-mahr rule is a fixed law of pre-consummation divorce. The mutʿa in case (1) is proportional — the verse explicitly says: "the wealthy according to his means, the poor according to his means."
Where the schools differ
Mutʿa status in case (1)Ḥanafī, Shāfiʿī: obligatory. Mālikī: strongly recommended but not strictly obligatory. Ḥanbalī: obligatory.
If wife was secluded with husband (khalwa) but no actual consummationḤanafī, Ḥanbalī (and Mālikī): the full mahr is owed — being secluded counts as effective consummation. Shāfiʿī: only actual physical consummation triggers full mahr.
Related verses
Q 4:4 (mahr is a free gift). Q 2:241 (mutʿa for all divorced women). Q 33:49 (similar pre-consummation rule for the Prophet's marriages).
Quran 2:238
Prayer Middle prayer
حَافِظُوا عَلَى الصَّلَوَاتِ وَالصَّلَاةِ الْوُسْطَىٰ وَقُومُوا لِلَّهِ قَانِتِينَ
"Maintain with care the prayers — especially the middle prayer — and stand devoutly before Allah."
Plain meaning
Establishing prayer is reiterated, with special emphasis on "the middle prayer" (al-ṣalāh al-wusṭā). The verse appears in the middle of the divorce/family law section — a reminder that even amid civil-law upheaval, the foundation of regular prayer must not be neglected.
Majority view
All schools agree on the verse's command to maintain prayer with care. The famous interpretive dispute is over which prayer is the "middle." The Prophet ﷺ said on the day of the Battle of the Trench: "They prevented us from the middle prayer, ʿaṣr" (Muslim) — the strongest evidence.
Where the schools differ
Ḥanafī, Mālikī, ShāfiʿīThe middle prayer is ʿAṣr (mid-afternoon). Cited Prophetic statement gives this view strong primacy.
Some Ḥanbalī, some early CompanionsThe middle prayer is Fajr (dawn) — between the night and the daylight prayers. Minority view today.
Practical impactNone — all schools require all five prayers regardless. Both Fajr and ʿAṣr are commonly missed, so both deserve extra attention.
Related verses
Q 4:103 (prayer at specified times — "decree of timed observance"). Q 2:43 (establish prayer). Q 17:78 (prayer times from declining sun to night).
Quran 2:240
Widow's provision Abrogated
وَالَّذِينَ يُتَوَفَّوْنَ مِنكُمْ وَيَذَرُونَ أَزْوَاجًا وَصِيَّةً لِّأَزْوَاجِهِم مَّتَاعًا إِلَى الْحَوْلِ غَيْرَ إِخْرَاجٍ ۚ فَإِنْ خَرَجْنَ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي مَا فَعَلْنَ فِي أَنفُسِهِنَّ مِن مَّعْرُوفٍ
"Those of you who die and leave widows — a bequest for their wives: one year's provision without expelling them. If they leave on their own, there is no blame on you for what they reasonably do for themselves."
Plain meaning
Originally, widows received one year of housing and maintenance from the deceased husband's estate, with the right to stay in the marital home. This was the pre-inheritance-shares legal arrangement.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools hold this verse was abrogated by two later revelations: (1) Q 2:234 shortened the ʿidda from one year to four months and ten days, and (2) Q 4:12 established the widow's fixed inheritance share (1/4 if no children, 1/8 if children). The widow no longer needs a separate year-long maintenance — her inheritance share secures her future.
Where the schools differ
Status of the verseAll four schools: abrogated in operative law. A minority of early scholars (and some modern ones) read the verse as still optionally applicable — i.e., the widow has a right to remain in the marital home if she chooses, even past the formal ʿidda. The majority hold this is replaced by the inheritance system.
Practical impactThe widow now: completes ʿidda of 4 months 10 days, may stay in the marital home during it, then has her own life and inherited share thereafter.
Related verses
Q 2:234 (4 months 10 days ʿidda — abrogating this one). Q 4:12 (widow's fixed inheritance share). Q 2:106 (the abrogation principle itself).
Quran 2:241
Mutʿa Divorce
وَلِلْمُطَلَّقَاتِ مَتَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۖ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُتَّقِينَ
"For divorced women, a provision in kindness — a duty upon the God-conscious."
Plain meaning
Beyond the mahr already paid, divorced women are entitled to a parting gift (mutʿa) from the former husband. The verse uses the language of obligation — "a duty upon the God-conscious" — not optional charity.
Majority view
All four schools recognize mutʿa as a divorce-related obligation, with differing strictness. Amount is "reasonable" — proportional to the husband's means. Not the same as ongoing maintenance during ʿidda (that's separate, Q 65:6); this is a parting recognition of the marriage that was.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīMutʿa is strictly obligatory for women divorced before consummation (combined with half mahr per Q 2:237). Recommended for others.
MālikīMutʿa is recommended (mandūb) in all cases — not strictly obligatory. The verse's "duty upon the God-conscious" reads as a moral imperative.
ShāfiʿīObligatory for pre-consummation divorces (like Ḥanafī). Recommended for others.
ḤanbalīObligatory in cases where no mahr at all was specified or paid. Otherwise recommended.
Related verses
Q 2:236–237 (mahr in pre-consummation divorce + recommendation of generosity). Q 33:28 (Prophet's wives — given mutʿa if they preferred worldly life). Q 65:6 (post-divorce maintenance during ʿidda).
Quran 2:245
Charity Reward
مَّن ذَا الَّذِي يُقْرِضُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَاعِفَهُ لَهُ أَضْعَافًا كَثِيرَةً ۚ وَاللَّهُ يَقْبِضُ وَيَبْسُطُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ
"Who is the one who will lend Allah a goodly loan, so He will multiply it many times over? Allah withholds and gives abundantly, and to Him you will return."
Plain meaning
The verse uses commercial language — "lending" to Allah — to frame charitable giving. Allah promises to multiply the "loan" many-fold. The metaphor is striking: the Creator borrowing from creation. The point: charity is the safest, highest-yielding investment imaginable.
Majority view
All schools cite this verse alongside Q 2:261 (the 700-fold parable). The conditions for a "goodly loan" (qarḍ ḥasan) are: (1) from halal earnings, (2) given without harm, reminders, or showing off, (3) for Allah's sake alone. The same Arabic phrase — qarḍ ḥasan — is also used in modern Islamic finance for interest-free loans to those in need.
Related verses
Q 2:261 (the 700-fold parable). Q 57:11 ("who will lend Allah a goodly loan"). Q 57:18 ("those who give in charity… it will be multiplied for them"). Q 64:17 (same promise).
Quran 2:256
Religious freedom No coercion
لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ ۖ قَد تَّبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ
"There is no compulsion in religion. The right path has become distinct from error."
Plain meaning
One of the most-cited verses in Islamic ethics. No one can be forced into Islam — sincere faith requires free choice. The reasoning given: truth has been made clear; people are capable of distinguishing it from error and are free to choose.
Majority view
All four schools accept this verse as operative law. Classical jurists used it to argue that forced conversion is invalid — even under Muslim rule, even of war captives. Children born to Muslim parents inherit the religion by lineage, but adults must come willingly. The principle establishes the moral floor of all Islamic legal practice: belief itself cannot be coerced.
Where the schools differ
Historical scopeClassical view: the verse applies in all places and times. Modern view (most): the verse is the foundational principle; classical expansionist jihad was about political subjugation, not forced conversion. Forced conversion was always invalid.
ApostasyClassical view: leaving Islam carried worldly penalty under specific conditions (state authority required). Modern view (growing majority): the Quran itself prescribes no worldly penalty for apostasy; the hadith on the matter applies to treason against the early state, not private belief.
Related verses
Q 18:29 ("Let whoever wills believe, whoever wills disbelieve"). Q 88:21–22 ("You are not over them a controller"). Q 109 (the entire surah on religious distinction without coercion).
Quran 2:261
Charity Reward
مَّثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنبُلَةٍ مِّائَةُ حَبَّةٍ ۗ وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَن يَشَاءُ
"The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is that of a seed that grows seven ears, each ear containing a hundred grains. Allah multiplies for whom He wills."
Plain meaning
A motivational parable. Each unit of charity is multiplied at minimum 700-fold (7 ears × 100 grains). And Allah multiplies even further for whom He wills. The Quran's most striking positive framing of charity — it is an investment with cosmic returns, not a loss.
Majority view
All schools cite this verse in fiqh discussions on charity. Classical scholars derived: charity must be approached with the orientation of investment, not loss. The 700× figure isn't a literal contract; it's the parable for divine generosity. The verse opens a long passage on the etiquette of giving (2:262–274).
Where the schools differ
ReadingAll four schools read this as motivational parable, not literal numerical guarantee. No legal dispute on the verse itself.
Threshold for "in the way of Allah"Schools agree it includes any genuine cause that pleases Allah — feeding the poor, supporting religion, helping family, public good. Funding obviously sinful causes does not qualify.
Related verses
Q 2:245 (whoever lends to Allah a goodly loan, He multiplies). Q 57:11, Q 64:17 (similar multiplication parables). Q 2:262 (the etiquette of giving that follows).
Quran 2:262
Charity etiquette No boasting
الَّذِينَ يُنفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ لَا يُتْبِعُونَ مَا أَنفَقُوا مَنًّا وَلَا أَذًى ۙ لَّهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ
"Those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah, and then do not follow it up with reminders [of their gift] or harm — their reward is with their Lord. They will not fear nor grieve."
Plain meaning
Charity must be followed by silence. Reminding the recipient of your generosity, mentioning your gift publicly to make them feel small, or causing them any harm through your manner of giving — all cancel the spiritual reward of the charity itself. The character of giving matters as much as the giving.
Majority view
All schools cite this verse as foundational. The Prophet ﷺ named three categories Allah will not speak to on the Day of Resurrection: "one who reminds others of his gifts; one who lengthens his garment in arrogance; and one who sells his goods by false oaths" (Muslim 106). The verse establishes the principle that intention and follow-through matter at least as much as the act of giving.
Where the schools differ
"Mann" (reminder of gift)All schools agree it is prohibited. Some scholars: even inwardly taking pride in the gift while seeing the recipient is a lesser form of mann. Best practice: give and forget.
"Adhā" (harm)Includes harsh tone, humiliation, expectation of public thanks. The harm doesn't have to be physical to nullify the charity.
Related verses
Q 2:264 (charity for show is void — same chapter, two verses later). Q 2:271 (hidden charity is better than public). Q 76:9 ("we feed you for Allah's sake alone — we want no reward or thanks").
Quran 2:264
Charity for show Sincerity
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُم بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ كَالَّذِي يُنفِقُ مَالَهُ رِئَاءَ النَّاسِ وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ۖ فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌ فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا
"O you who believe, do not nullify your charity by reminders or harm — like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in Allah and the Last Day. He is like a smooth rock covered with dust; a heavy rain strikes it and leaves it barren."
Plain meaning
Two ways charity is voided: (1) following it up with reminders or harm (covered already in 2:262), (2) giving to be seen by others. The vivid metaphor: such charity is like a thin layer of dust on smooth rock — the first heavy rain washes it away, leaving nothing. The visible act remains; the spiritual investment evaporates.
Majority view
All schools cite this verse as a foundational warning against riyāʾ (showing off) in any act of worship. The act itself is not invalidated for the recipient — the poor person still receives the money — but the giver loses the spiritual reward entirely. Sincerity must be present at the moment of giving and maintained afterward.
Where the schools differ
"Showing off" — what countsClassical: giving primarily to gain public reputation. Modern: includes social-media-driven charity that is structured to be witnessed; opening prayers structured for public approval; visible donor recognition that becomes the actual motivation.
Mixed intentionsWhat if someone gives partly sincerely and partly to be seen? Classical: the showing-off portion at least cancels its share of reward; the sincere portion may remain. The Prophet ﷺ warned heavily against riyāʾ as "the hidden shirk" — even at the smallest level.
Related verses
Q 2:262 (no reminders or harm — paired with this). Q 4:38 (spending wealth to be seen by people — companions of Satan). Q 76:9 ("we feed you for Allah's sake alone — we want no reward or thanks").
Quran 2:267
Quality of charity
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنفِقُوا مِن طَيِّبَاتِ مَا كَسَبْتُمْ وَمِمَّا أَخْرَجْنَا لَكُم مِّنَ الْأَرْضِ ۖ وَلَا تَيَمَّمُوا الْخَبِيثَ مِنْهُ تُنفِقُونَ وَلَسْتُم بِآخِذِيهِ إِلَّا أَن تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ
"Spend from the good of what you have earned, and from what We have brought forth for you from the earth. Do not deliberately give the bad of it — you yourselves would not accept it except with closed eyes."
Plain meaning
Do not give as charity what you wouldn't accept yourself. If you wouldn't wear it, eat it, or use it — don't give it. The verse calls out the practice of treating charity as a disposal mechanism for inferior or unwanted items. The standard is: comparable quality to what you'd use.
Majority view
All schools treat this as a foundational principle of charity ethics. Charity must come from the ṭayyibāt (the good), not the inferior. The verse explicitly names the hypocrisy: you yourselves wouldn't accept that, so why give it?
Where the schools differ
Practical scopeAll schools agree on the principle. Differences are in detail — e.g., is giving away clothing slightly worn (but otherwise fine) acceptable? All schools: yes. Giving worn-out, stained, or broken items? Discouraged across schools.
Modern applicationDonating expired food, ruined items, or anything beneath your own usability standard violates this principle. Modern dignity-focused charity (e.g., choice-based food banks) align with this verse better than traditional in-kind donation of unwanted goods.
Related verses
Q 3:92 ("you will not attain piety until you spend from what you love"). Q 76:8–9 (feeding the needy "from love of it, for Allah's sake alone"). Q 2:264 (charity nullified by harm or boasting).
Quran 2:271
Hidden charity
إِن تُبْدُوا الصَّدَقَاتِ فَنِعِمَّا هِيَ ۖ وَإِن تُخْفُوهَا وَتُؤْتُوهَا الْفُقَرَاءَ فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ ۚ وَيُكَفِّرُ عَنكُم مِّن سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ
"If you disclose your charity, it is well — but if you conceal it and give it to the poor, that is better for you. It will erase some of your bad deeds."
Plain meaning
Both public and private giving are good. Public can encourage others. But private giving is better for two reasons: it preserves the dignity of the recipient (no public association with poverty), and it removes any temptation of showing off in the giver.
Majority view
All schools cite this verse in the etiquette of giving. The Prophet ﷺ named the seven types of person shaded by Allah on the Day of Resurrection — among them: "one who gives charity so secretly that his left hand does not know what his right hand spends" (Bukhari 1423, Muslim 1031). The bar for "secret" is high: not even acknowledging the gift to oneself in any prideful way.
Where the schools differ
When public giving is preferableAll schools agree the public mode is valid and sometimes preferable: to encourage a cause, set a public example, or shame others into generosity. Default in private life: hidden.
Zakāt vs. ṣadaqaZakāt is the obligatory floor and can be paid more openly without violating the principle. Voluntary ṣadaqa more strongly benefits from being hidden.
Related verses
Q 2:262 (no reminding or harm). Q 2:264 (charity for show is void). Q 76:8–9 (feeding the needy for Allah's sake alone). Q 4:38 (those who spend to be seen by others — Satan's companions).
Quran 2:273
Hidden poverty
لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الَّذِينَ أُحْصِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ ۖ تَعْرِفُهُم بِسِيمَاهُمْ لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا
"[Charity is] for the poor who are restricted in Allah's path, unable to travel for trade — the ignorant think them rich because of their dignity. You recognize them by their bearing; they do not ask people insistently."
Plain meaning
A specific category of the genuinely poor: those who are too dignified to beg, so much so that the casual observer mistakes them for prosperous. The verse describes them as visible to discerning eyes — their bearing reveals the truth — but invisible to the careless. Such people deserve charity that is actively sought out, not waited upon to be asked for.
Majority view
All schools cite this as the basis for actively seeking out hidden poverty. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The poor person is not the one who goes round begging for a morsel or two — the real poor is the one who does not find enough to live on yet is not noticed so that he might be given charity" (Bukhari). The principle: don't wait for people to ask; learn who in your community needs help quietly.
Related verses
Q 2:271 (hidden charity is better — pairs with this verse). Q 9:60 (the eight categories of zakat recipients including the poor). Q 51:19 (in their wealth was a right for the beggar and the deprived).
Quran 2:275
Transactions Ribā
الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ الرِّبَا لَا يَقُومُونَ إِلَّا كَمَا يَقُومُ الَّذِي يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا الْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ الرِّبَا ۗ وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا
"Those who consume usury will not stand on the Day of Resurrection except as one stands who has been struck by Satan's touch. That is because they said, 'Trade is just like usury' — but Allah has permitted trade and forbidden usury."
Plain meaning
Ribā (literally "increase") — taking interest on loans — is categorically forbidden. The verse rejects the pre-Islamic Arab argument that interest is "just like" profit from trade. They are not the same: trade involves real risk and real exchange; interest is guaranteed return on lent money.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools agree ribā is among the gravest sins. Two main types: (1) ribā al-nasīʾa — interest on loans, time-based, the main modern concern; (2) ribā al-faḍl — unequal exchange of the same commodity (e.g., bartering different grades of dates by weight unequally). Both forbidden.
Where the schools differ
Which commodities ribā al-faḍl applies toḤanafī: things sold by weight or measure. Shāfiʿī: foodstuffs + currency. Mālikī: foodstuffs that can be stored + currency. Ḥanbalī: similar to Shāfiʿī.
Modern bankingMainstream contemporary view (across schools): conventional bank interest is ribā. Alternative Islamic finance instruments (murābaḥa, ijāra, sukuk) are designed to replace it.
Necessity exceptionsSome modern scholars permit conventional banking for Muslims in non-Muslim countries where Islamic alternatives are unavailable, under necessity (ḍarūra). Others reject this.
Related verses
Q 2:276 (Allah destroys ribā and increases charity), 2:278–279 (give up what remains of ribā after this revelation; otherwise it is war from Allah and His Messenger), 3:130 (do not consume ribā doubled and multiplied), 4:161 (taking ribā was forbidden to Jews too).
Quran 2:276
Ribā Baraka
يَمْحَقُ اللَّهُ الرِّبَا وَيُرْبِي الصَّدَقَاتِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ كَفَّارٍ أَثِيمٍ
"Allah destroys ribā and gives increase to charities. Allah does not love any disbeliever or persistent sinner."
Plain meaning
A striking inversion. Wealth gained through ribā looks like it grows — but Allah erodes it (numerically or in baraka, blessing). Wealth given as charity looks like it shrinks the bank balance — but Allah makes it grow. The same Arabic root r-b-w means "to grow" — Allah inverts what truly grows what.
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse as a foundational statement on the spiritual character of wealth. Numerical growth is not the measure of true wealth — baraka (divine blessing) is. The verse pairs with Q 2:275 (ribā is forbidden) and Q 2:278–279 ("war from Allah"). Three verses together establish the full Quranic ribā doctrine.
Related verses
Q 30:39 ("your gift made looking to grow does not grow with Allah — but zakat does"). Q 2:275 (ribā forbidden). Q 2:278–279 (war from Allah on those who persist).
Quran 2:278–279
Ribā Repentance
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَذَرُوا مَا بَقِيَ مِنَ الرِّبَا إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ ۝ فَإِن لَّمْ تَفْعَلُوا فَأْذَنُوا بِحَرْبٍ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ۖ وَإِن تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ
"O you who believe, fear Allah and give up what remains of ribā, if you are believers. If you do not — then expect war from Allah and His Messenger. But if you repent, you may have your principal — neither wronging nor being wronged."
Plain meaning
Among the strongest warnings in the Quran. Any remaining ribā transactions must be abandoned immediately. Persistence after this warning = war from Allah and His Messenger — extraordinary language reserved for only the gravest sins. The repentance terms are restorative: keep your principal; forfeit all accrued interest. Neither party is wronged.
Majority view
One of only two sins for which the Quran explicitly declares "war from Allah and His Messenger" (the other is hostility toward His allies in battle). All four schools cite this verse as closing any remaining debate on ribā's permissibility — even for transactions already underway. The provision balances justice both ways: no theft from the lender (principal returned), no continued exploitation of the borrower (interest forgiven).
Where the schools differ
Modern bankingMainstream contemporary fatwa across all four schools: conventional bank interest is ribā. Islamic finance instruments (murābaḥa, ijāra, mushāraka, sukuk) are designed as ribā-free alternatives.
Necessity exception for minoritiesSome modern scholars permit limited engagement with conventional banking for Muslims in non-Muslim-majority countries where Islamic alternatives are unavailable, citing ḍarūra. Others firmly reject all such exceptions.
Pre-existing ribā at conversionAll schools: a new convert with existing interest-based debts receives a transition. The principal is owed but the interest portion is forgiven going forward.
Related verses
Q 2:275 (just above — ribā vs trade). Q 2:276 (Allah destroys ribā, multiplies charity). Q 3:130 (doubled and multiplied ribā). Q 4:161 (ribā was forbidden to the Jews too).
Quran 2:280
Debt mercy Forgiveness
وَإِن كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ ۚ وَأَن تَصَدَّقُوا خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ
"If the debtor is in hardship, then grant him time until ease. And if you give up [the debt] as charity, that is better for you — if only you knew."
Plain meaning
A creditor must not press a debtor in genuine financial distress for repayment. Grant them time until they can pay. Going further — forgiving the debt entirely as charity — is treated as the highest form of generosity. The verse comes within the ribā prohibition section: the contrast is sharp — creditors should be merciful, not extract more.
Majority view
All four schools require granting grace periods to debtors in genuine hardship. Demanding payment from someone who cannot pay is forbidden. Forgiving the debt is the highest expression of charity and produces extraordinary reward. This verse is the Quranic foundation of Islamic bankruptcy and debt-relief jurisprudence.
Where the schools differ
"Hardship" — proof needed?Schools agree: the debtor's claim of hardship must be credible. Mālikī: visible signs of poverty are enough. Ḥanafī, Shāfiʿī: courts may verify; if dispute, the creditor may take an oath that he believes the debtor able.
Duration of grace"Until ease" — schools agree this is not a fixed time. Modern application: bankruptcy proceedings, restructuring agreements, formal extensions are all consistent with this verse.
Zakat status of forgivenessSome scholars: forgiving a debt counts toward zakat obligation for the year. Others: separate from zakat. Mixed views.
Related verses
Q 2:275 (ribā forbidden). Q 9:60 (debtors among the eight zakat recipients). Q 4:5–6 (don't give wealth to incompetent managers).
Quran 2:282
Debt Contracts Witnesses
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا تَدَايَنتُم بِدَيْنٍ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى فَاكْتُبُوهُ ۚ وَلْيَكْتُب بَّيْنَكُمْ كَاتِبٌ بِالْعَدْلِ … وَاسْتَشْهِدُوا شَهِيدَيْنِ مِن رِّجَالِكُمْ ۖ فَإِن لَّمْ يَكُونَا رَجُلَيْنِ فَرَجُلٌ وَامْرَأَتَانِ
"O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down. Let a scribe write between you justly. … Bring two male witnesses; if not two men, then one man and two women — so that if one errs, the other may remind her."
Plain meaning
The longest verse in the Quran (~129 words) — a detailed contract-law passage. Rules for credit/debt arrangements: write everything down; use a neutral scribe; record the amount, terms, and due date; bring witnesses; both parties must agree to the recorded version. Designed to prevent disputes about repayment.
Majority view
All four Sunni schools agree on the documentation requirement (recommended but not strictly obligatory — the principle is to have evidence). The verse's witness configuration — two men, or one man + two women — has been one of the most-debated provisions in modern Islamic legal thought.
Where the schools differ
Witness ratio scopeClassical position (all four schools): the 1m+2w ratio is the general rule for commercial testimony. The verse explains the rationale: less commercial experience in 7th-century context made memory reinforcement prudent.
Modern interpretationMany modern scholars (Rashīd Riḍā, al-Qaraḍāwī, others): the ratio is context-bound — it depended on commercial competence at the time, not gender per se. Equally competent witnesses count equally today. This is a real debate in contemporary fiqh.
Other testimony domainsWitness requirements differ by context: 4 male witnesses for adultery (24:4), 2 witnesses for wills (5:106), women's sole testimony accepted for women-specific matters (childbirth, breastfeeding). The 1m+2w ratio is not a universal Quranic rule.
Related verses
Q 2:283 (alternative to written contracts when traveling). Q 4:135 (general justice in testimony). Q 5:106 (witnesses to wills). Q 24:4 (witnesses for slander accusation).
Quran 2:283
Pledge Trust
وَإِن كُنتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَّقْبُوضَةٌ ۖ فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُم بَعْضًا فَلْيُؤَدِّ الَّذِي اؤْتُمِنَ أَمَانَتَهُ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ
"If you are on a journey and find no scribe, a pledge taken in hand will do. If one of you entrusts another, let the one entrusted discharge his trust and fear Allah his Lord."
Plain meaning
Continuation of 2:282. Where written contracts aren't feasible (travel, urgency, no available scribe), a physical pledge (rahn) or held security substitutes. And in any case — even without paperwork — the entrusted party must honor the trust as a matter of God-consciousness, not just legal pressure.
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse as the foundation of Islamic security/collateral law (kitāb al-rahn). Schools agree: the pledge must be transferred ("taken in hand") for the security to be legally constituted. The verse closes with a reminder that legal forms are secondary — God-consciousness governs trust whether or not a contract exists.
Where the schools differ
"Taken in hand" — physical possessionḤanafī (classical strict): the lender must take actual physical possession of the pledge for the security to bind. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: legal possession (constructive) is sufficient in some cases. Modern banking: registered title/legal pledge widely accepted.
Use of the pledged itemḤanafī: lender may not use the pledged item. Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī: may use it if doing so doesn't damage it and the parties agree (e.g., a pledged animal that needs to be ridden, a pledged garden that needs harvesting).
If pledge perishesAll schools have detailed rules. General principle: lender bears the loss of the pledge while it is in his keeping, but the debt remains owed.
Related verses
Q 2:282 (the long debt verse — context of this one). Q 4:58 (return trusts to their owners). Q 8:27 (do not betray Allah, the Messenger, or your trusts).

Plain meaning
Love of Allah is shown through obedience to His Messenger ﷺ. The two are inseparable in Islamic ethics. The verse continues that turning away from this obedience equates with disbelief — establishing a high-stakes link between the claim of faith and the act of following the prophetic guidance.
Majority view
All four schools cite this as one of the foundational verses for the authority of the Sunnah (Prophetic example) as a source of law. The Quran pairs obedience to Allah with obedience to the Messenger in over 60 instances. Following the Prophet ﷺ — in his teaching, his judgments, and his example — is itself a Quranic obligation, not merely a recommendation.
Related verses

Quran 3:92

"You will never attain true righteousness until you spend from what you love. Whatever you spend — Allah is aware of it."
Plain meaning
Real virtue is parting with what you actually value. Giving from leftovers, unwanted items, or excess doesn't reach the height. The verse explicitly raises the bar: the threshold for "true righteousness" (birr) is sacrifice of something genuinely treasured.
Majority view
All schools cite this verse. When revealed, the companion Abū Ṭalḥa immediately gave away his most beloved property — an orchard called Bayrūḥāʾ — saying he could not be a person who failed this test. The Prophet ﷺ approved. The verse sets the gold standard for sincerity in charity.
Related verses
Q 2:261 (700-fold parable). Q 2:267 (don't give bad-quality charity). Q 76:8–9 (feeding the needy "out of love" for the food, for Allah's sake alone).
Quran 3:97
Hajj Sanctuary of Makkah
فِيهِ آيَاتٌ بَيِّنَاتٌ مَّقَامُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ ۖ وَمَن دَخَلَهُ كَانَ آمِنًا ۗ وَلِلَّهِ عَلَى النَّاسِ حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ مَنِ اسْتَطَاعَ إِلَيْهِ سَبِيلًا ۚ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ عَنِ الْعَالَمِينَ
"In it are clear signs — the standing-place of Abraham; whoever enters it is safe. Pilgrimage to the House is owed to Allah by people — by whoever is able to find a way to it. Whoever disbelieves — Allah is free of need from all the worlds."
Plain meaning
This is the verse that makes Hajj obligatory. The wording is striking: it is a debt owed to Allah (lillāhi ʿalā n-nāsi) by every person capable of reaching the Kaʿba. Refusal is equated with disbelief — showing how seriously this duty is taken.
Majority view
All schools: Hajj is the fifth pillar, obligatory once in a lifetime on every adult Muslim with physical and financial capacity (istiṭāʿa) and a safe route. The conditions of "ability" include: sound mind, sufficient provision for the journey AND for dependents left behind, safe roads, and (for women, classically) a maḥram or trustworthy company.

ḤanafīHajj must be performed immediately (on the year capacity is attained); delaying is sinful. A woman needs a maḥram or husband for the journey if it's longer than 3 days' travel.

ShāfiʿīHajj is tarākhī — may be delayed within one's lifetime as long as one is reasonably confident of fulfilling it later. Woman needs maḥram or trustworthy company.
ḤanbalīImmediacy required, like Ḥanafī/Mālikī. Maḥram required for woman; without one, Hajj is not yet obligatory on her.
Related verses

"Hold firmly together to the rope of Allah and do not be divided. Remember Allah's favor upon you — when you were enemies and He united your hearts, so that by His grace you became brothers."
Plain meaning
Unity among believers is commanded; division is condemned. The "rope of Allah" is identified by classical commentators as: the Quran, the religion of Islam, the community of believers. The verse reminds the original Medinan audience of pre-Islamic tribal warfare between Aws and Khazraj — healed only after Islam.
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse as the foundational text on Muslim unity. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd said: "The rope of Allah is the Quran." Holding to one shared scripture defines the umma as one community, regardless of jurisprudential differences.
Where the schools differ
Permissible vs impermissible differencesAll schools agree: differences in jurisprudence (the four Sunni schools differing on details) are valid and do not constitute "division." Sectarian declarations that one Muslim group is "outside Islam" without grounds — this is what the verse forbids.
Threshold for "division"Classical: declaring another Muslim outside Islam, refusing to pray with them, or refusing to attend their funerals. Modern: extends to partisan attitudes that fracture communities along ethnic or political lines.
Related verses
Q 8:46 (do not dispute, lest you lose courage). Q 30:32 (do not be among those who split their religion into sects). Q 49:9–10 (reconcile between fighting believers — they are brothers).

"Let there arise from among you a community calling to good, commanding right, and forbidding wrong. Those are the successful ones."
Plain meaning
The Quranic duty of amr bi-l-maʿrūf wa-nahy ʿan al-munkar — "enjoining good and forbidding wrong." Some Muslims must actively work for the spread of good and the prevention of evil. Three components: (1) calling to good (positive promotion), (2) commanding what's right (correction), (3) forbidding what's wrong (active resistance to evil).
Majority view
All four schools treat this as a foundational social duty. The famous hadith elaborates: "Whoever sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith" (Muslim 49). Three levels of resistance corresponding to one's capacity.
Where the schools differ
Collective vs individual obligationḤanafī and most others: collective obligation (farḍ kifāya) — fulfilled if some Muslims discharge it. Mālikī: closer to individual obligation in proportion to capacity.
MethodAll schools: wisdom and gradation are required. The hand-tongue-heart hadith applies according to one's authority and means.
Modern applicationCivil society work, religious instruction, social activism, journalism that calls out injustice — all forms of this duty.
Related verses
Q 9:71 (men and women guardians of one another, commanding right). Q 3:110 ("you are the best community brought forth — you enjoin good and forbid wrong"). Q 31:17 (Luqmān's advice to his son).

Majority view
All four schools agree: this verse is part of the gradual Quranic prohibition of ribā. It specifically condemns the worst form (compound interest), but the full prohibition extends to all interest — not just compounding — based on the categorical language of Q 2:275.

Plain meaning
Testing in wealth (loss, taxes, debts, taking it from you) and selves (illness, persecution, death of loved ones, verbal abuse) is certain — the verse uses lām al-qasam (oath particle). Foundational verse on the theology of suffering in Islam. All schools cite it to teach that hardship is structural, not anomalous. The legal corollary: one cannot avoid duties on the grounds that they are difficult — ṣabr is the prescribed response, not abandonment.
Related verses

Quran 3:200
Patience Ribāṭ Closing of Surah
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اصْبِرُوا وَصَابِرُوا وَرَابِطُوا وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ
"O you who believe! Be patient, outdo others in patience, hold fast (ribāṭ), and be mindful of Allah — so that you may succeed."
Plain meaning
The closing verse of Surah Āl ʿImrān, listing four imperatives: (1) iṣbirū — be patient, (2) ṣābirū — outdo the opposition in patience, (3) rābiṭū — hold the line / be steadfast / stand guard, (4) taqwā — God-consciousness. The promised result: falāḥ — success in both worlds.
Majority view
"Rābiṭū" has both a military and a spiritual reading. Classically: stationing oneself at the frontier (ribāṭ) to defend Muslim lands. The Prophet ﷺ said one night of ribāṭ is better than a month of fasting and prayer (Muslim). Spiritual reading: holding firm in prayer, "tying" oneself to one prayer after another. All four schools accept both readings as legitimate; both apply.
Where the schools differ
Classical militaryRibāṭ = stationing at the frontier (thaghr) of Muslim territory to defend against invasion. Considered one of the noblest acts. Schools differ on minimum duration (Mālikī: 40 days; others: any time).
Spiritual / SūfīRibāṭ = binding the heart to remembrance of Allah, awaiting the next prayer, guarding oneself against sin. The Prophet's ﷺ statement: "Shall I not tell you of that by which Allah erases sins and raises ranks? Performing wuḍūʾ properly in unpleasant conditions, taking many steps to the mosque, and waiting for prayer after prayer — that is the ribāṭ" (Muslim).
Related verses

Quran 4:1
Single origin Wombs / kinship
يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ اتَّقُوا رَبَّكُمُ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُم مِّن نَّفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ وَخَلَقَ مِنْهَا زَوْجَهَا وَبَثَّ مِنْهُمَا رِجَالًا كَثِيرًا وَنِسَاءً ۚ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ الَّذِي تَسَاءَلُونَ بِهِ وَالْأَرْحَامَ
"O mankind, be mindful of your Lord — who created you from a single soul, created from it its mate, and from the two of them scattered many men and women. Be mindful of Allah — by whom you ask one another — and of the wombs (kinship)."
Plain meaning
The opening verse of An-Nisāʾ — the surah on family, inheritance, and women's rights — establishes the theological foundation: all humanity descends from one soul. Equality of origin grounds equality of dignity. The verse ends with two parallel commands: fear Allah and fear (violating) the wombs — meaning kinship ties.
Majority view
All four schools cite this as the basis for two related obligations: (1) ṣilat al-raḥim — maintaining kinship ties is a binding duty, cutting them is a major sin; (2) kinship inheritance — blood relations have specified, non-negotiable rights to inherit. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The womb is suspended from the Throne saying, 'whoever maintains me, Allah maintains; whoever cuts me, Allah cuts'" (Bukhārī).
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīThe phrase "al-arḥām" (wombs) by itself is one of the strongest oaths; swearing falsely "by the wombs" is a binding oath that, if broken, requires kaffāra.
Mālikī/Shāfiʿī/ḤanbalīGenerally: only oaths by Allah's names or attributes are binding. Swearing by "the wombs" is discouraged but not a legal oath. Ḥanafīs are alone in the binding view.
Related verses
Q 49:13 (We made you peoples and tribes to know one another — humanity from one source). Q 47:22–23 (those who cut their kinship are cursed). Q 7:189 (created you from one soul and made from it its mate).
Quran 4:3
Polygamy Justice Orphans
وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تُقْسِطُوا فِي الْيَتَامَىٰ فَانكِحُوا مَا طَابَ لَكُم مِّنَ النِّسَاءِ مَثْنَىٰ وَثُلَاثَ وَرُبَاعَ ۖ فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا فَوَاحِدَةً
"If you fear you will not be just with the orphans (under your care), then marry those women who are lawful to you — two, three, or four. But if you fear you cannot deal justly, then only one."
Plain meaning
Context: revealed after Uḥud when many men had died and orphan girls were vulnerable to exploitation by guardians who married them for their property without giving proper mahr. The verse offers a remedy: if you fear injustice toward orphan girls, marry from the wider pool of women — up to four — with the explicit condition of justice. If you cannot be just, marry only one. The default is one wife.
Majority view
All four schools: polygamy is permitted, not encouraged, and strictly capped at four wives simultaneously. Justice (ʿadl) between wives in time, accommodation, and finances is obligatory. Read with Q 4:129 ("you will never be able to be perfectly just between women") — emotional equality is impossible and not required; material equality is required. A man who cannot be materially just is forbidden multiple wives.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīIf a man marries a fifth while still married to four, the fifth marriage is fāsid (irregular). If he divorces one then marries another, he must wait for her ʿidda to end first.
Shāfiʿī/ḤanbalīSimilar — fifth marriage is invalid (bāṭil). After divorcing one, the man may marry a new wife after her ʿidda ends.
MālikīSimilar position. Mālikīs additionally permit a woman to stipulate in the marriage contract that her husband not take another wife — and if he does, she may divorce.
Modern reformistSome modern states (Tunisia, Turkey) have effectively banned polygamy by reading Q 4:3 alongside Q 4:129 as requiring impossible justice. The four classical schools all reject this reading.
Related verses
Q 4:129 ("you will never be able to be just between women, however much you desire"). Q 4:127 (orphan girls and their rights). Q 33:50 (special exception for Prophet ﷺ — beyond four).
Quran 4:4
Mahr / dowry Marriage
وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً ۚ فَإِن طِبْنَ لَكُمْ عَن شَيْءٍ مِّنْهُ نَفْسًا فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَّرِيئًا
"Give women their dowries as a free gift. But if they willingly give up some of it to you, then consume it with pleasure and ease."
Plain meaning
Mahr (also ṣadāq) is paid by the husband to the wife, not to her father. The word niḥla means "a gift, freely given" — emphasizing that it is hers absolutely. If she chooses, voluntarily and without pressure, to give some of it back or use it for the household, that is permissible. Pressure or expectation of return invalidates that consent.
Majority view
All four schools: mahr is an essential requirement of a valid marriage contract — though it may be deferred (muʾakhkhar) or paid in installments. There is no fixed minimum or maximum in the Quran. The wife owns her mahr completely; the husband or her family have no claim on it. Forcing or pressuring her to return it is a violation of this verse.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīMinimum mahr: 10 dirhams (silver coins) or equivalent. A contract specifying less is corrected upward to 10 dirhams.
MālikīMinimum: 3 dirhams (or 1/4 dīnār). Below this, the marriage may be invalidated until proper mahr is paid.
Shāfiʿī/ḤanbalīNo minimum. Any object of value — even an iron ring or teaching of Quran — is valid (based on the ḥadīth of Sahl: the Prophet ﷺ married a couple where mahr was teaching some Quran).
Related verses
Q 4:24 (give the dowries due to them as an obligation). Q 4:20–21 (if you wish to replace one wife with another, do not take back even a heap of gold from her). Q 2:237 (mahr in divorce before consummation).
Quran 4:6
Orphans' property Maturity test
وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُم مِّنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا أَن يَكْبَرُوا ۚ وَمَن كَانَ غَنِيًّا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ ۖ وَمَن كَانَ فَقِيرًا فَلْيَأْكُلْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
"Test orphans (in maturity) until they reach marriageable age. If you find them rationally competent (rushd), hand over their property to them. Do not consume it wastefully or in haste before they grow up. The rich (guardian) should abstain from it; the poor may consume what is reasonable."
Plain meaning
Three sequential rulings on managing orphans' property: (1) test them before releasing the property — see if they can handle money responsibly; (2) once they reach marriageable age AND show rushd (financial soundness), release their property to them; (3) guardians must not exploit the wealth — wealthy guardians charge nothing; poor guardians may take only reasonable compensation for managing it.
Majority view
All schools: orphan's property is held in trust. Releasing it requires two conditions met together: bulūgh (puberty/adulthood) AND rushd (responsible judgment). Failing either, the property continues to be held. Guardians who consume the wealth wrongfully are committing one of the major sins explicitly named in the Quran (see Q 4:10 — "consuming fire").
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīIf rushd is not present at puberty, property is held until age 25. After 25, property must be released even if rushd is still doubtful — adulthood overrides.
Mālikī/Shāfiʿī/ḤanbalīBoth bulūgh AND rushd are required without age cap. If rushd is never attained, the property is held indefinitely under guardianship.
Poor guardian's wageAll schools allow a poor guardian reasonable compensation. Ḥanafī: limited to actual labor cost. Shāfiʿī: lesser of customary wage or actual cost.
Related verses
Q 4:2 (do not exchange their good property for your bad). Q 4:10 (those who consume orphan's wealth consume only fire into their bellies). Q 6:152 (do not approach orphan's property except in the best way). Q 17:34 (same).
Quran 4:7
Inheritance principle Women inherit
لِّلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِّمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ مِّمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ مِمَّا قَلَّ مِنْهُ أَوْ كَثُرَ ۚ نَصِيبًا مَّفْرُوضًا
"For men is a share of what parents and close relatives leave, and for women is a share of what parents and close relatives leave — be it little or much — an obligatory share."
Plain meaning
This is the verse that abolished pre-Islamic inheritance law, under which only adult males who could fight inherited. Women, children, and the elderly inherited nothing. The verse establishes three principles in a single sentence: (1) women inherit, (2) regardless of estate size, (3) the share is obligatory (mafrūḍan) — not discretionary.
Majority view
All four schools cite this as the foundational verse for ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ — the science of inheritance shares — said by the Prophet ﷺ to be "half of all knowledge" (Ibn Māja). The estate is not a gift the deceased can withhold from heirs; it is a right Allah has assigned. Wills (waṣiyya) may dispose only of up to 1/3 of the estate, and not to existing heirs.
Related verses
Q 4:11–12 (detailed shares of children, spouses, parents). Q 4:13–14 (those who keep these limits enter Paradise; those who transgress them enter Fire). Q 4:33 (heirs we have appointed). Q 2:180 (writing a will for parents and relatives — abrogated for heirs by 4:11).
Quran 4:11
Inheritance shares Children Parents
يُوصِيكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي أَوْلَادِكُمْ ۖ لِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنثَيَيْنِ ۚ فَإِن كُنَّ نِسَاءً فَوْقَ اثْنَتَيْنِ فَلَهُنَّ ثُلُثَا مَا تَرَكَ ۖ وَإِن كَانَتْ وَاحِدَةً فَلَهَا النِّصْفُ ۚ وَلِأَبَوَيْهِ لِكُلِّ وَاحِدٍ مِّنْهُمَا السُّدُسُ مِمَّا تَرَكَ إِن كَانَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ
"Allah commands you concerning your children: for the male, the equivalent of the share of two females. If there are only daughters — two or more — they get two-thirds of what is left. If only one daughter, she gets half. For each parent, one-sixth of what is left, if the deceased had children..."
Plain meaning
The most famous and most misunderstood inheritance ruling. The default — when sons and daughters both exist — is that each son receives twice the share of each daughter. This is not a statement of relative worth but reflects the asymmetry of legal financial obligations: sons must support wife, children, parents, sometimes siblings; daughters have no mandatory financial obligations and keep their full mahr and wealth.
Majority view
All four schools agree on the literal application: li-l-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi l-unthayayn applies only when male and female children of the same level (e.g., sons and daughters of the deceased) coexist. In many other configurations, women receive equal to or more than men (mother vs father; sister vs brother of the maternal line — Q 4:12 — both get 1/6). The rule of 2:1 covers a minority of inheritance scenarios.
Where the schools differ
Order of distributionAll four schools: (1) burial expenses, (2) debts, (3) up to 1/3 for non-heir bequests, (4) then remaining estate distributed per Quran shares.
ʿAwl (overlap reduction)Sunni schools: if assigned shares add to more than 1, all shares reduced proportionally. Jaʿfarī (Shia): reduce from the daughter's share instead — leads to different distributions in some cases.
GrandchildrenMālikī/Shāfiʿī/Ḥanbalī: grandchildren whose parent died before the grandparent are excluded by living uncles (classical rule). Modern Egyptian/Tunisian/Pakistani law: introduced "obligatory bequest" to grandchildren — controversial but widespread.
Related verses
Q 4:12 (spouse and sibling shares). Q 4:13 ("these are the limits set by Allah"). Q 4:176 (kalāla — inheritance with no parents or children).
Quran 4:12
Spouse share Kalāla Inheritance
وَلَكُمْ نِصْفُ مَا تَرَكَ أَزْوَاجُكُمْ إِن لَّمْ يَكُن لَّهُنَّ وَلَدٌ ۚ فَإِن كَانَ لَهُنَّ وَلَدٌ فَلَكُمُ الرُّبُعُ ۚ ... وَلَهُنَّ الرُّبُعُ مِمَّا تَرَكْتُمْ إِن لَّمْ يَكُن لَّكُمْ وَلَدٌ ۚ فَإِن كَانَ لَكُمْ وَلَدٌ فَلَهُنَّ الثُّمُنُ
"You (men) get half of what your wives leave, if they have no child; if they have a child, you get one-quarter... They (wives) get one-quarter of what you leave, if you have no child; if you have a child, they get one-eighth..."
Plain meaning
The verse completes the inheritance formula. Husband's share: 1/2 (no child) or 1/4 (with child). Wife's (or wives' shared) share: 1/4 (no child) or 1/8 (with child). The verse then addresses kalāla — a deceased who left no parents and no children — giving siblings 1/6 each (later overridden by Q 4:176 for full siblings).
Majority view
All four schools: spousal shares are fixed, not optional. "Child" (walad) in the verse includes both sons and daughters, and includes children from any spouse — not only the surviving spouse's own children. Multiple wives share the wife's portion equally — not each receive 1/4 or 1/8 separately. Same applies in kalāla: the 1/6 for "one brother or one sister" refers to half-siblings from the mother's side (per consensus, traced to Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd).
Where the schools differ
Half-siblings from motherAll schools agree: this verse's "brother/sister" means maternal half-siblings. Full siblings and paternal half-siblings are addressed in 4:176 with different shares.
Wives sharing one portionIf a man dies with 4 wives and no child: all 4 share the 1/4 — meaning each gets 1/16. All schools agree.
Radd (return)If shares add to less than the estate (no residual heirs), Ḥanafī/Ḥanbalī: remainder returned to share-holders proportionally. Mālikī/Shāfiʿī (classical): remainder goes to bayt al-māl (treasury). Modernized in most Muslim states.
Related verses
Q 4:11 (children/parent shares). Q 4:176 (full and paternal sibling kalāla). Q 4:13–14 (warning to respect these limits).
Quran 4:19
Treatment of wives Forced marriage banned
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا يَحِلُّ لَكُمْ أَن تَرِثُوا النِّسَاءَ كَرْهًا ۖ وَلَا تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ لِتَذْهَبُوا بِبَعْضِ مَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ ... وَعَاشِرُوهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۚ فَإِن كَرِهْتُمُوهُنَّ فَعَسَىٰ أَن تَكْرَهُوا شَيْئًا وَيَجْعَلَ اللَّهُ فِيهِ خَيْرًا كَثِيرًا
"O believers, it is not lawful for you to inherit women against their will, nor to restrict them so as to take back part of what you have given them ... Live with them in kindness. If you dislike them — perhaps you dislike something in which Allah has placed much good."
Plain meaning
The verse abolishes a pre-Islamic Arab practice: when a man died, his heirs (often a son from another wife) would claim his widow as inheritance — sometimes marrying her against her will or holding her hostage until she paid for release. This is now ḥarām. Three prohibitions in one verse: (1) inheriting women as property, (2) restricting them to extract back their mahr, (3) mistreating them. The positive command: ʿāshirūhunna bi-l-maʿrūf — "live with them in kindness".
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse as the basis for several rulings: (1) women cannot be inherited or forced into marriage — consent (riḍā) is required; (2) husband cannot harass a wife to make her seek khulʿ (giving back mahr to leave) — that is exploitation; (3) good companionship is a positive obligation, not merely the absence of harm. Modern reading extends this to emotional and verbal kindness, not only financial fairness.
Related verses
Q 4:21 (you cannot take back the mahr — even "a heap of gold"). Q 2:231 (do not hold them to harm them). Q 2:229 (divorce twice; then either retain in kindness or release in kindness). Q 30:21 (made between you love and mercy).
Quran 4:22–23
Prohibited marriages Maḥārim
وَلَا تَنكِحُوا مَا نَكَحَ آبَاؤُكُم مِّنَ النِّسَاءِ ... حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمْ أُمَّهَاتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُكُمْ وَأَخَوَاتُكُمْ وَعَمَّاتُكُمْ وَخَالَاتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُ الْأَخِ وَبَنَاتُ الْأُخْتِ وَأُمَّهَاتُكُمُ اللَّاتِي أَرْضَعْنَكُمْ وَأَخَوَاتُكُم مِّنَ الرَّضَاعَةِ ...
"Do not marry women your fathers married ... Forbidden to you are: your mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal aunts, maternal aunts, brothers' daughters, sisters' daughters, the mothers who nursed you, your sisters through nursing, the mothers of your wives, your stepdaughters under your care from wives you have entered, the wives of your sons of your own loins, and that you join two sisters in marriage..."
Plain meaning
The comprehensive list of maḥārim — women a man can never marry. Three categories: (1) blood relations — mother, daughter, sister, aunts (both sides), nieces; (2) nursing relations — milk-mother and milk-sisters; the principle: "what is forbidden by birth is forbidden by nursing"; (3) in-law relations — mother-in-law (absolutely), stepdaughter (if marriage to her mother was consummated), daughter-in-law, plus the prohibition on joining two sisters as co-wives.
Majority view
All four schools: this list is comprehensive and final. The 14 categories of forbidden women + 4 in 4:24 = the full list. Nursing (raḍāʿ) creates the same prohibitions as blood when it meets the conditions: occurred in infancy. Step-relations through wife: mother-in-law is forbidden permanently even if the marriage was never consummated; stepdaughter only if the marriage was consummated.
Where the schools differ
Number of nursingsḤanafī/Mālikī: any amount of nursing (even one swallow) creates the prohibition. Shāfiʿī/Ḥanbalī: minimum 5 separate nursings required (based on Ḥadīth of ʿĀʾisha in Muslim).
Age cutoff for nursingḤanafī: nursing within 2.5 lunar years. Mālikī: 2 years + a small grace. Shāfiʿī/Ḥanbalī: strict 2 lunar years. After cutoff, no prohibition is created.
Father's lawful intercourse partnersAll schools: a woman lawfully possessed by the father is also forbidden — including (in classical law) a slave-concubine. This is no longer practically relevant.
Related verses
Q 4:24 (continuation: also forbidden are married women, except what your right hand possesses). Q 24:31 (list of male maḥārim before whom a woman may unveil). Q 33:50 (the women lawful to the Prophet ﷺ).
Quran 4:24
Marriage validity Mahr obligation
وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ إِلَّا مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ ۖ ... وَأُحِلَّ لَكُم مَّا وَرَاءَ ذَٰلِكُمْ أَن تَبْتَغُوا بِأَمْوَالِكُم مُّحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ ۚ فَمَا اسْتَمْتَعْتُم بِهِ مِنْهُنَّ فَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً
"And (forbidden also) are married women, except those (captives) whom your right hand possesses ... Beyond these, all others are lawful to you, that you may seek them with your wealth in honest wedlock, not in fornication. As to those with whom you have consummated marriage, give them their dowries as a duty (farīḍa)."
Plain meaning
Closes the prohibited list — married women are forbidden; all other women (outside the prohibited categories above) are lawful for marriage. The verse then states three conditions: (1) seek marriage with your wealth — meaning paying mahr; (2) muḥṣinīn — in honest wedlock, not casual relations; (3) the mahr after consummation is farīḍa — an obligation, not optional.
Majority view
All four schools: marriage requires (1) two parties who are not in the prohibited categories, (2) consent, (3) two witnesses (Ḥanafī requires Muslim witnesses; others vary), (4) mahr (a value, even minimal). The phrase fa-mā istamtaʿtum bihi in this verse is used by Twelver Shia as the basis for mutʿa (temporary marriage) — universally rejected by the four Sunni schools, who hold that mutʿa was permitted briefly and then abrogated by the Prophet ﷺ (ḥadīth of ʿAlī, Muslim).
Where the schools differ
Sunni four schoolsMutʿa (temporary marriage with a set end-date) is ḥarām. The verse refers to ordinary marriage; istamtaʿtum = "the enjoyment of marriage", not a special temporary form.
Twelver Shia (Jaʿfarī)Mutʿa is lawful with conditions: explicit duration, explicit mahr, no inheritance, no maintenance. Cite this verse plus a different reading of the abrogation reports.
"Married women" + captivesClassical schools allowed marriage to captured women whose pagan husbands were left behind — applied in conquest contexts. Inoperative today; the principle that married women are otherwise forbidden remains absolute.
Related verses
Q 4:25 (those who cannot afford to marry free women may marry believing maids). Q 2:235 (do not contract marriage during ʿidda). Q 5:5 (lawful to marry chaste women of the People of the Book).
Quran 4:29
Wrongful taking Mutual consent Suicide forbidden
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ إِلَّا أَن تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَن تَرَاضٍ مِّنكُمْ ۚ وَلَا تَقْتُلُوا أَنفُسَكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِكُمْ رَحِيمًا
"O believers, do not consume one another's wealth in falsehood, unless it be by trade with mutual consent. And do not kill yourselves — Allah is most merciful to you."
Plain meaning
Two foundational principles in one verse: (1) wealth: all property transfers between Muslims must be either lawful (sale, gift, inheritance) or by mutual consent in trade — anything else is bāṭil; (2) life: do not kill yourselves, and (per the majority) do not kill one another, since Muslims are "one body".
Majority view
All four schools cite this as the foundational verse of commercial law. From it derive: validity of sales, prohibition of fraud, prohibition of forcing transactions, prohibition of riba (consumption without exchange), legitimacy of khiyār (right to cancel). The closing phrase grounds the prohibition of suicide (and, in fiqh, self-mutilation, refusing necessary medical care to the point of death, etc.). The Prophet ﷺ: "Whoever kills himself with something in this world will be tortured with it on the Day of Resurrection" (Bukhārī, Muslim).
Related verses
Q 2:188 (do not consume wealth between you wrongfully or use it to bribe judges). Q 4:161 (taking ribā while forbidden). Q 2:195 (do not throw yourselves into destruction by your own hands). Q 5:32 (whoever kills a soul, it is as if he killed all mankind).
Quran 4:32
No envy Earned share
وَلَا تَتَمَنَّوْا مَا فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ بِهِ بَعْضَكُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ ۚ لِّلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِّمَّا اكْتَسَبُوا ۖ وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ مِّمَّا اكْتَسَبْنَ ۚ وَاسْأَلُوا اللَّهَ مِن فَضْلِهِ
"Do not covet that by which Allah has favored some of you over others. To men is a share of what they have earned, and to women is a share of what they have earned. Ask Allah of His bounty."
Plain meaning
Revealed (per ʿĀʾisha and Umm Salama) when women asked the Prophet ﷺ why men get larger inheritance shares and the chance to fight in jihād. The verse responds: don't envy what Allah has differently assigned. Then it states something rarely emphasized: both men and women have their own share of their own earnings. A woman's earned wealth is hers, not her husband's or guardian's.
Majority view
All four schools: a woman's earnings, inheritance, and mahr are her absolute property. The husband has no claim. She has no obligation to spend on household expenses (his obligation); any contribution she makes is voluntary. The verse also grounds the principle: instead of envying others' shares, supplicate Allah for His bounty in your own life.
Related verses
Q 4:7 (women inherit obligatorily). Q 2:233 (the father is responsible for maintenance of mother and child). Q 65:7 (let the man of means spend according to his means).
Quran 4:34
Qiwāma Nushūz Marital discipline
الرِّجَالُ قَوَّامُونَ عَلَى النِّسَاءِ بِمَا فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَبِمَا أَنفَقُوا مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ ۚ ... وَاللَّاتِي تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ فَعِظُوهُنَّ وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ وَاضْرِبُوهُنَّ ۖ فَإِنْ أَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا
"Men are qawwāmūn over women by what Allah has favored some over others and by what they spend of their wealth ... Those (wives) from whom you fear nushūz (severe disloyalty / mistreatment): admonish them, separate from them in beds, and (then) strike them. If they obey you, seek no further course against them."
Plain meaning
The most debated verse in the Quran. Qawwām ≠ "ruler" — it carries the sense of one who maintains, protects, manages financially. The reasoning is given in the verse: it is grounded in the man's financial obligation (maintenance, mahr) — not inherent superiority. Nushūz here is serious wifely disloyalty (not minor disagreement). The verse outlines a three-step escalation: (1) verbal advice, (2) separation in sleeping arrangements, (3) ḍarb. The Prophet ﷺ himself never struck a woman, and ḥadīth specify if any "strike" occurs it must be non-injurious, not on the face, and "with a siwāk (twig)".
Majority view
All four classical schools: the three-step process is sequential — admonition first, then separation in bed, then a symbolic, non-harming strike only as a last resort to save the marriage from breakdown. Striking that injures (bruise, broken skin, fear) is universally ḥarām — it constitutes ḍarar (harm) and gives the wife grounds for judicial divorce with full mahr. Modern scholars (Ibn ʿĀshūr, Ramadan al-Būṭī, contemporary Mālikīs) emphasize the verse permits nothing more than symbolic gesture and many argue the third step is functionally inoperative since the conditions (non-harm, last resort, no anger) make it nearly impossible to discharge correctly.
Where the schools differ
All four classical schoolsPermission for non-injurious strike exists in principle as a last-resort measure. Hitting that causes any mark or injury is sinful and grounds for criminal liability and judicial divorce.
Modern reformistMany read iḍribūhunna as "separate from them" (a less common but linguistically valid meaning of ḍ-r-b, as in "ḍaraba fī l-arḍ" — travel). On this reading, the three steps are: words, bed-separation, full separation.
Application todaySeveral Muslim-majority states (Tunisia, Morocco family code reforms) and most contemporary jurists hold that any striking of a wife is impermissible given universally evolved standards of harm.
Related verses
Q 4:19 (live with them in kindness). Q 30:21 (between you love and mercy). Q 2:228 (men have a degree over them — contextually about divorce procedure, not absolute superiority). Q 4:128 (when a husband shows nushūz — the verse uses the same word symmetrically).
Quran 4:35
Marital arbitration Family mediation
وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا فَابْعَثُوا حَكَمًا مِّنْ أَهْلِهِ وَحَكَمًا مِّنْ أَهْلِهَا إِن يُرِيدَا إِصْلَاحًا يُوَفِّقِ اللَّهُ بَيْنَهُمَا
"If you fear a breach between the two (spouses), appoint an arbiter from his family and an arbiter from her family. If they (the arbiters / couple) intend reconciliation, Allah will bring them to harmony."
Plain meaning
Before divorce: mandatory mediation. One arbiter from each side — meaning, not "lawyers" but trusted family members who know the spouses and will negotiate in good faith. The purpose is iṣlāḥ (reconciliation), not legal partisanship.
Majority view
All four schools: arbitration is required before judicial intervention, when reconciliation has failed at the couple level. The arbiters' role: investigate, recommend, and (in classical Mālikī view) bind the couple to their judgment, including divorce if irreconcilable. This verse is the basis for modern family courts in Muslim-majority countries requiring mediation before granting divorce.
Where the schools differ
Ḥanafī/ShāfiʿīThe arbiters are advisors only. Their recommendation, even to divorce, requires the husband's consent (since divorce belongs to him).
Mālikī/ḤanbalīThe arbiters have binding authority. They may decree separation if reconciliation is impossible — even without the husband's consent — and the wife receives full mahr.
Related verses
Q 4:128 (if a woman fears nushūz from her husband — reconciliation is best). Q 49:9–10 (between two parties — reconcile them with justice).
Quran 4:36
Rights of others Parents Neighbors
وَاعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُشْرِكُوا بِهِ شَيْئًا ۖ وَبِالْوَالِدَيْنِ إِحْسَانًا وَبِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْجَارِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْجَارِ الْجُنُبِ وَالصَّاحِبِ بِالْجَنبِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ وَمَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ
"Worship Allah and associate nothing with Him. Be good to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, the near neighbor, the distant neighbor, the companion at your side, the wayfarer, and those whom your right hand possesses (dependents)."
Plain meaning
A complete map of obligations to others in one verse, ranked from closest (parents) outward: parents → relatives → orphans → poor → near neighbor → distant neighbor → companion at one's side → traveler → dependents. The opening pairs worship of Allah with excellent treatment (iḥsān) of these groups — they are presented together as parts of one duty.
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse for: (1) birr al-wālidayn — kindness to parents is an absolute obligation; (2) silat al-raḥim — kinship ties; (3) ḥuqūq al-jār — neighbor's rights (the Prophet ﷺ said Jibrīl repeatedly emphasized neighbors to him until he thought they would inherit). "Companion at the side" = travel companion / spouse / coworker — anyone in close proximity. "Wayfarer" grounds hospitality obligations.
Related verses
Q 17:23–24 (do not say "uff" to parents — lower wing of humility). Q 31:14 (carried in weakness upon weakness). Q 2:177 (true piety includes spending on relatives, orphans, the needy).
Quran 4:43
Intoxication ban (stage 2) Tayammum
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَقْرَبُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنتُمْ سُكَارَىٰ حَتَّىٰ تَعْلَمُوا مَا تَقُولُونَ ... وَإِن كُنتُم مَّرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِّنكُم مِّنَ الْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا
"O believers, do not approach prayer while intoxicated, until you know what you are saying ... If you are ill, on a journey, or have come from relieving yourselves, or have had intimate contact with women, and find no water — then perform tayammum with pure earth..."
Plain meaning
The second of three stages restricting alcohol — earlier Q 2:219 said "in them is some benefit but greater sin"; this verse bans prayer while drunk; Q 5:90 finally bans alcohol entirely. The verse also legislates tayammum — dry purification with clean earth when water is unavailable, sick, or harmful — a permanent ease (rukhṣa).
Majority view
All four schools accept that this alcohol prohibition was superseded — alcohol is now banned at all times, not just before prayer (per Q 5:90). On tayammum: required by all schools when water is absent, when water would harm the user's health, or when water is far enough that seeking it would cause harm or missing prayer. Lāmastum ("touched"): differing interpretations across schools.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīLāmastum = intercourse only. Mere skin contact between man and woman does not break wuḍū. Tayammum: one strike on earth, wipes face and arms.
ShāfiʿīLāmastum = any skin contact between non-maḥram man and woman breaks wuḍū. Tayammum: two strikes — one for face, one for arms to elbows.
MālikīLāmastum = touch with desire breaks wuḍū; incidental touch does not. Tayammum: one strike, may extend to wrists only as obligatory; up to elbows is recommended.
ḤanbalīSimilar to Mālikī (touch with desire); tayammum to wrists.
Related verses
Q 2:219 (alcohol — some benefit but greater sin). Q 5:90–91 (final alcohol prohibition). Q 5:6 (parallel tayammum verse).
Quran 4:48
Shirk unforgivable Tawḥīd
إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَغْفِرُ أَن يُشْرَكَ بِهِ وَيَغْفِرُ مَا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَن يَشَاءُ ۚ وَمَن يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ افْتَرَىٰ إِثْمًا عَظِيمًا
"Allah does not forgive that anything be associated with Him, but forgives anything less than that for whom He wills. Whoever associates anything with Allah has fabricated a tremendous sin."
Plain meaning
A foundational verse of Islamic theology with direct legal implications. Shirk — assigning partners or rivals to Allah — is the only sin Allah will not forgive if one dies upon it. All other sins, however major, are forgivable. The verse implicitly clarifies: anyone who turns from shirk and repents is forgiven — the prohibition is on dying in shirk.
Majority view
All schools and creeds: this verse is the legal foundation for treating shirk as the ultimate violation. From it derive: invalidity of inheritance between a Muslim and a non-Muslim relative (classical view), inadmissibility of testimony from polytheists in Muslim courts (classical view), the obligation to invite to tawḥīd as the highest priority. It also grounds Islamic optimism about forgiveness — every other sin, however large, is forgivable.
Related verses
Q 4:116 (verse is repeated almost verbatim — emphasizing the rule). Q 31:13 (shirk is the great wrong — ẓulm ʿaẓīm). Q 39:53 (do not despair of Allah's mercy — He forgives all sins). Q 25:68–70 (those who repent — Allah will replace their sins with good deeds).
Quran 4:58
Trusts (amāna) Just rulership
إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَن تُؤَدُّوا الْأَمَانَاتِ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا وَإِذَا حَكَمْتُم بَيْنَ النَّاسِ أَن تَحْكُمُوا بِالْعَدْلِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ نِعِمَّا يَعِظُكُم بِهِ
"Allah commands you to return trusts (amānāt) to their owners, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice. Excellent indeed is what Allah counsels you."
Plain meaning
Two foundational duties: (1) amāna — anything entrusted to you (money, secrets, public office, responsibilities) must be returned to its proper owner; (2) ʿadl — judging between people, in any capacity, must be done with justice. Revealed (per most reports) about the key to the Kaʿba, which the Prophet ﷺ returned to its non-Muslim custodian even after conquering Makkah.
Majority view
All four schools regard this as a foundational verse of governance and judicial ethics. From amāna: public office is a trust, embezzlement is a major sin, deposits must be returned even to enemies, secrets shared in confidence must be kept. From ʿadl: judges must be impartial regardless of party — including against their own kin, against the powerful, against fellow Muslims if the case favors the non-Muslim. Q 4:135 reinforces: "be witnesses in justice, even against yourselves, parents, or kin".
Related verses
Q 4:135 (stand firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even against yourselves). Q 5:8 (do not let hatred of a people lead you to injustice — be just; that is closer to taqwā). Q 38:26 (judge between people in truth, do not follow desire). Q 33:72 (the amāna was offered to the heavens — humans accepted it).
Quran 4:59
Obedience Authority Dispute resolution
يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ ۖ فَإِن تَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ فَرُدُّوهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ إِن كُنتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَحْسَنُ تَأْوِيلًا
"O believers, obey Allah, obey the Messenger, and those in authority among you. If you dispute over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger — if you truly believe in Allah and the Last Day. That is better and more excellent in outcome."
Plain meaning
The foundational verse of Islamic governance. Three levels of authority in descending order: (1) Allah (the Quran), (2) the Messenger ﷺ (Sunnah), (3) ūlū l-amr — those given authority (rulers, scholars). Note the wording: "obey Allah and obey the Messenger" — but for ūlū l-amr, no separate "and obey" — meaning their obedience is conditional on the first two. When disputes arise, the appeal is back to Allah (Quran) and the Messenger (Sunnah) — not to ūlū l-amr.
Majority view
All four schools: obedience to legitimate rulers is obligatory in matters of obedience to Allah. The Prophet ﷺ: "There is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator" (Aḥmad). Disputes over legal interpretation are resolved by appeal to Quran and Sunnah — this is the verse that establishes ijtihād (independent reasoning from sources) as the method when sources do not yield a clear answer. Ūlū l-amr, in classical readings, includes both political rulers and scholars (ʿulamāʾ) — Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid favored scholars; others, rulers.
Where the schools differ
Sunni schoolsObedience to a ruler is required even if unjust, as long as he does not order disobedience to Allah and the basic markers of Islam are maintained — to avoid civil war (fitna). Rebellion is permitted only against open kufr (visible disbelief in office).
Mūʿtazila / classical KhārijīsObedience to an unjust ruler is itself wrong; commanding right against him is obligatory even by force.
Twelver Shia"Ūlū l-amr" = the infallible imams. Disputes are resolved through them. Different basis for governance entirely.
Related verses
Q 3:32 (obey Allah and the Messenger). Q 4:80 (whoever obeys the Messenger obeys Allah). Q 24:54 (if you obey the Messenger, you will be rightly guided). Q 42:38 (their affairs are by mutual consultation).
Quran 4:65
Submission to Prophet's judgment Faith
فَلَا وَرَبِّكَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ حَتَّىٰ يُحَكِّمُوكَ فِيمَا شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُوا فِي أَنفُسِهِمْ حَرَجًا مِّمَّا قَضَيْتَ وَيُسَلِّمُوا تَسْلِيمًا
"By your Lord, they cannot truly believe until they make you judge in what disputes them, then find no resistance in themselves to what you have decided, and submit with complete submission."
Plain meaning
A startlingly direct verse on the conditions of faith. Three layers in ascending order: (1) making the Prophet ﷺ the judge (external submission), (2) internal acceptance with no lingering resentment (heart-submission), (3) full taslīm — total submission (will-submission). Anything short of all three falls short of true belief.
Majority view
All four schools: this verse grounds the obligation to accept the rulings of the Quran and Sunnah, even where they conflict with personal preference. It is foundational for the legitimacy of Sunnah as law-bearing — not merely advice. The verse was revealed (per most reports) about a Companion who disputed with Zubayr over irrigation; when the Prophet ﷺ ruled in Zubayr's favor, the man resented it. The verse warns that such resentment itself is incompatible with faith.
Related verses
Q 33:36 (no choice for any believer when Allah and His Messenger have decided). Q 24:51 (the saying of believers is: we hear and we obey). Q 4:115 (whoever opposes the Messenger after guidance is clear...).
Quran 4:80
Authority of the Messenger Sunnah
مَّن يُطِعِ الرَّسُولَ فَقَدْ أَطَاعَ اللَّهَ ۖ وَمَن تَوَلَّىٰ فَمَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ عَلَيْهِمْ حَفِيظًا
"Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah. And whoever turns away — We have not sent you over them as a guardian."
Plain meaning
A short, decisive verse. Obedience to the Messenger ﷺ is obedience to Allah — they cannot be separated. Likewise, disobedience to the Messenger is disobedience to Allah. The Prophet's ﷺ role, however, ends with delivering the message; he is not the cosmic enforcer of compliance — that is Allah's domain.
Majority view
All four schools and the entire Sunni tradition: this verse establishes the binding nature of the Sunnah. A Muslim who accepts the Quran but rejects authentic ḥadīth claims to obey Allah while disobeying the Messenger — the verse explicitly forbids this separation. From it derives the entire science of ḥadīth as a source of law equal in authority to the Quran (though Quran's verses are more certain in transmission). Quranists who reject ḥadīth are universally considered outside the Sunni mainstream.
Related verses
Q 59:7 (what the Messenger gives you, take; what he forbids you, abstain from). Q 33:21 (in the Messenger of Allah is an excellent example). Q 4:69 (those who obey Allah and the Messenger are with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, the righteous).
Quran 4:92–93
Manslaughter Murder Diya / Kaffāra
وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ أَن يَقْتُلَ مُؤْمِنًا إِلَّا خَطَأً ۚ وَمَن قَتَلَ مُؤْمِنًا خَطَأً فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُّؤْمِنَةٍ وَدِيَةٌ مُّسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ ... وَمَن يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُّتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ خَالِدًا فِيهَا وَغَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا
"It is not for a believer to kill another believer — except by mistake. Whoever kills a believer by mistake: the freeing of a believing slave and blood-money (diya) paid to the victim's family ... Whoever kills a believer intentionally: his recompense is Hell, dwelling therein forever — Allah's anger and curse are upon him, and a great torment is prepared for him."
Plain meaning
The Quran's most direct legislation on homicide. Manslaughter (khaṭāʾ) — accidental killing — requires: (1) kaffāra (atonement) of freeing a believing slave (today: 60 days of consecutive fasting per the parallel kaffāra pattern); (2) diya — blood-money paid to the victim's heirs unless they remit it. Intentional murder (ʿamd) — its threat in the Quran is the most severe of any sin: eternal Hell, Allah's wrath, His curse. Sunni scholars qualify "eternal" as not literally permanent for a believer who repents.
Majority view
All four schools categorize homicide into three (or sometimes five) types: (1) ʿamd — intentional, with a typically-lethal weapon: qiṣāṣ (retaliation) or diya if family forgives; (2) shibh ʿamd — quasi-intentional, with a typically non-lethal instrument: diya only, no qiṣāṣ (per Ḥanafī/Ḥanbalī; Mālikī collapses this into intentional); (3) khaṭāʾ — accidental: kaffāra + diya. Diya is paid by the killer's ʿāqila (paternal kin / today: insurance equivalent), not the killer alone — in classical fiqh.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīThree categories (intentional, quasi-intentional, accidental). Diya for full intentional homicide is paid only if family chooses pardon over qiṣāṣ.
MālikīTwo main categories — intentional or accidental. Quasi-intentional folded into intentional or accidental depending on context.
Shāfiʿī/ḤanbalīSimilar three categories as Ḥanafī. Diya amount: 100 camels (or equivalent) for a free Muslim male. Female: half. Non-Muslim under Muslim protection: varies (1/3 to full, depending on school).
Related verses
Q 2:178–179 (qiṣāṣ established). Q 5:32 (whoever kills a soul... it is as if he killed all mankind). Q 17:33 (do not kill a soul except by right; the heir of the murdered has authority).
Quran 4:101–103
Travel prayer Fear prayer
وَإِذَا ضَرَبْتُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَلَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَن تَقْصُرُوا مِنَ الصَّلَاةِ ... وَإِذَا كُنتَ فِيهِمْ فَأَقَمْتَ لَهُمُ الصَّلَاةَ فَلْتَقُمْ طَائِفَةٌ مِّنْهُم مَّعَكَ ... فَإِذَا اطْمَأْنَنتُمْ فَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ ۚ إِنَّ الصَّلَاةَ كَانَتْ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ كِتَابًا مَّوْقُوتًا
"When you travel through the land, there is no blame on you if you shorten the prayer ... When you are with them and lead them in prayer, let a party of them stand with you ... When you are at ease, establish prayer in full. Prayer is, for the believers, prescribed at fixed times."
Plain meaning
Three rulings: (1) qaṣr — shortening of four-rakʿa prayers to two during travel; (2) ṣalāt al-khawf — fear/battlefield prayer, performed in shifts so half the group can still keep watch; (3) prayer is kitāban mawqūtan — an obligation at fixed times that cannot be neglected even in war.
Majority view
All four schools: qaṣr applies on a journey meeting minimum distance (varies). Some hold it is obligatory (Ḥanafī); others recommended permission (Shāfiʿī, Mālikī, Ḥanbalī — the traveler may pray in full but qaṣr is preferred). Praying is required even on the battlefield — but in a modified form. The principle: no situation justifies skipping prayer entirely.
Where the schools differ
ḤanafīQaṣr is obligatory, not optional, for travelers. Minimum travel distance: ~88 km. Traveler who prays full is sinful.
MālikīQaṣr is a strongly emphasized Sunnah, almost obligatory. Minimum: ~85 km.
ShāfiʿīQaṣr is permission, not obligation. Minimum: ~88 km. Praying full is permissible.
ḤanbalīPermission. Minimum: ~88 km. Combining (jamʿ) Ẓuhr+ʿAṣr or Maghrib+ʿIshāʾ is also permitted on journey.
Related verses
Q 2:239 (if you fear, then pray walking or riding). Q 4:43 (do not approach prayer while drunk). Q 11:114 (establish prayer at the two ends of the day).
Quran 4:114
Secret counsel When private talk is good
لَّا خَيْرَ فِي كَثِيرٍ مِّن نَّجْوَاهُمْ إِلَّا مَنْ أَمَرَ بِصَدَقَةٍ أَوْ مَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ إِصْلَاحٍ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ ۚ وَمَن يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ فَسَوْيُفَ نُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا
"There is no good in much of their secret counsels — except for one who urges charity, kindness, or reconciliation between people. Whoever does this seeking the pleasure of Allah, We shall give him an immense reward."
Plain meaning
A precision rule for speech, particularly najwā — private/secret conversations. Most of what people share in private is harmful — gossip, plotting, complaint. Three exceptions where private speech is good: (1) urging charity, (2) urging maʿrūf (right conduct), (3) reconciling between people — and even then, only if motivated by seeking Allah's pleasure, not social positioning.
Majority view
All four schools cite this verse on the ethics of speech and the special status of mediation. Notably: iṣlāḥ bayna n-nās (peacemaking) is described in Sunnah as superior in reward to nawāfil prayer and fasting. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Shall I tell you of something greater in rank than prayer, fasting, and charity? Reconciling between people, for resentment is the shaver — it shaves religion, not hair" (Tirmidhī). The verse also explicitly permits strategic deception in the context of reconciliation: lying to bring two estranged people together is not considered lying.
Related verses
Q 58:9 (when you do consult privately, do not consult about sin and aggression — only about righteousness and taqwā). Q 49:9–10 (between two parties of believers, reconcile them justly). Q 4:128 (reconciliation is best — between spouses).
